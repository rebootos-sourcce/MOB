## 1. William James (1842-1910)

**Who and why.** American psychologist and philosopher who judged inner experience by what it does in a life. He is the lens for "does this actually change anything?"

**Background and history.** Born January 11, 1842, in New York City, son of the theologian Henry James Sr. He died August 26, 1910, in New Hampshire. He trained in medicine at Harvard and taught there for most of his life: anatomy and physiology from 1873, psychology from 1875, philosophy from 1879. The turning point that cost him was a collapse in 1869-1870, deep enough that he considered suicide. After reading the French philosopher Charles Renouvier, he wrote in his diary on April 30, 1870: "My first act of free will shall be to believe in free will." Historians disagree about how much that one entry explains. Work helped too: in 1872 Harvard made him an instructor in physiology. Key works: "What is an Emotion?" (journal *Mind*, 1884), *The Principles of Psychology* (1890), *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (Gifford Lectures, Edinburgh, 1901-1902, published 1902), *Pragmatism* (1907), *The Meaning of Truth* (1909) and *Essays in Radical Empiricism* (published 1912, after his death).

**How he thinks.**
- *Body first.* In 1884 he reversed common sense. The body changes first, on seeing the exciting fact, and "our feeling of the same changes as they occur IS the emotion." This became the James-Lange theory of emotion.
- *The fruits test.* In *Varieties* he judged religious experience by its effects in a life, not by where it came from: "By their fruits ye shall know them, not by their roots."
- *Radical empiricism* (paraphrase of his 1909 preface): only things that can be defined in terms drawn from experience are open to philosophical debate. Things beyond experience may exist, but they are not material for argument.
- *Cases before theory.* *Varieties* is built on a large collection of first-person accounts, including strange ones, read side by side.

**Quality bar.**
1. Cash value: name the concrete difference a claim makes in someone's week. If there is none, the claim is decoration.
2. Body before label: the description starts with what the body does, then names the feeling.
3. Experience only: every claim points to something a person could notice in themselves.
4. Many cases: one vivid testimony is a story. A pattern across many people is evidence.
5. Origin does not decide value: a strange source does not sink an experience, and a holy source does not save it.

**Lens questions.**
- What does the reader do differently on Tuesday because of this page?
- Where is the experience this claim rests on, and could the reader find it in themselves?
- Is this judged by its fruits, or defended by its roots (Lance's story, a tradition, the formula)?
- Does the sentence start with the body or with a label?
- How many people does this pattern hold for, and how do we know?

**Applied to Lance's work.**
- Atüned: how intense a release session felt is the root. The fruit is what changed afterward. If the only record of a session is how it felt in the moment, this lens asks for a later check on what is different, in the person's own words.
- Book: where a chapter explains *why* an address holds stress, add one passage on what changes in daily life when it releases, described in terms the reader can test.
- Book: James would treat collected reader accounts as data, misses included. Many plain accounts beat one perfect one.

**Bridge to Lance's canon.** Overlap: James put feeling in the body in 1884, which is the ground a somatic map stands on. His fruits test fits Lance's "precision because mechanical" stance: judge by result. Differences: James never mapped stress to body locations and never proposed release through reading. His emotion theory is about the moment of feeling, not stored load. His radical empiricism would also ask, of each term in CQ = Intention x Integrity / Resistance, what a person directly experiences that the term names.

- The James-Lange theory is a historical landmark, not current consensus. Emotion science has moved on, and it has critics.

## 2. Carl Gustav Jung (1875-1961)

**Who and why.** Swiss psychiatrist who mapped the disowned parts of the psyche. He is the lens for what a piece of work leaves in the dark.

**Background and history.** Jung was Freud's closest early ally. His book *Wandlungen und Symbole der Libido* (1911-1912, later *Symbols of Transformation*) treated libido as general psychic energy, not only sexual drive. It also argued for a layer of the unconscious that all humans share, which he later called the collective unconscious. Both points split him from Freud. Their personal relationship ended in early 1913, by mutual consent, and he resigned the presidency of the International Psychoanalytical Association in April 1914. The break cost him his mentor, his place in the movement and, for some years, his footing. He called 1912-1918 his "confrontation with the unconscious" and recorded its images in the *Red Book* (*Liber Novus*). He worked on it from about 1914 to 1930, and it was published only on October 7, 2009 (Norton, edited by Sonu Shamdasani). A second, darker chapter: in 1933 he became president of the General Medical Society for Psychotherapy and editor of its journal while it operated under Nazi Germany. A December 1933 editorial in that journal praised Nazi ideology. Jung said he took the post to protect Jewish colleagues and that the editorial went in without his consent. Critics point to his 1934 writing on "Aryan" and "Jewish" psychology. This is still contested.

**How he thinks.**
- *Active imagination* (paraphrase of "The Transcendent Function", written 1916, published 1957): give an unconscious content a form (an image, writing, a dialogue, movement), then meet it consciously as a real other. He warned the method has dangers and should be used under expert supervision where possible.
- *Shadow*: the parts of the personality a person does not accept as their own. "Everyone carries a shadow, and the less it is embodied in the individual's conscious life, the blacker and denser it is." (*Psychology and Religion*, 1938, Collected Works 11, para. 131.) Secondary sources note the shadow is not necessarily evil.
- *Making it conscious*: "One does not become enlightened by imagining figures of light, but by making the darkness conscious." ("The Philosophical Tree", 1945, Collected Works 13, para. 335.)
- *Individuation* (paraphrase): the lifelong process of becoming a whole person by bringing the conscious and unconscious into relation, without either one wiping out the other. "Transcendent function" is his name for that union.

**Quality bar.**
1. What is excluded? Every clean picture has a shadow. Name it.
2. Integration over removal: the work helps a person own what they find, not only get rid of it.
3. The image is data: a dream, symbol or felt image is engaged, not explained away.
4. Opposites held: light and dark both stay in view.
5. Depth with a container: deep material is opened with a safeguard.

**Lens questions.**
- What does this piece refuse to look at, in the reader or in itself?
- Is what surfaces treated only as damage, or also as a lost capacity?
- Where does the light vocabulary (coherence, alignment) cover the hard material?
- Is the reader invited into a dialogue with what comes up, or told what it means?
- What holds the person when something deep opens?

**Applied to Lance's work.**
- Book: if distortion reads only as damage to clear, this lens marks it. Jung's shadow also holds disowned strength. One worked example where what surfaces is something the reader *wants back* would answer it.
- Atüned: if a coherence score can only reward feeling better, it rewards looking away. Check that a session where something dark surfaces and is simply acknowledged counts as a real result.
- Book: when a reader meets a strong image while reading, the text should say what to do with it (write it, speak to it) instead of interpreting it for them.

**Bridge to Lance's canon.** Lance says distortion is what Jung called the shadow. Overlap: both describe something disowned that works outside awareness and shapes behaviour until it is made conscious, and both use the language of energy. Differences: Jung's shadow is psychological content, a part of the personality, not stress stored at a body address. His libido is psychic energy, a psychological idea, not a physical quantity. His aim is integration (owning the shadow), where Lance's word is release. Jung's shadow carries positive potential, which "distortion" does not obviously allow. Treating shadow, Freud's ego, allostatic load and inflammation as one thing is Lance's synthesis. None of these authors made that claim, and Jung's shadow is not Freud's ego.

- Not re-confirmed this session: his birth and death dates (1875 Kesswil, 1961 Küsnacht) and his first meeting with Freud (1907). These are standard.
- Many popular "Jung quotes" online are paraphrases or inventions. Use only quotes tied to a Collected Works paragraph.

## 3. Bruce S. McEwen (1938-2020)

**Who and why.** Neuroendocrinologist who turned "stress wears you down" into measurable biology. He is the lens for whether a claimed cost is real and counted.

**Background and history.** He studied chemistry at Oberlin, took his PhD at Rockefeller University in 1964 and stayed at Rockefeller for more than 50 years. He was associate dean and then dean of its graduate programme from 1985 to 1993, and died January 2, 2020, aged 81. In 1968 his lab found that the rat hippocampus, a memory structure, is a target of adrenal stress hormones. That finding opened the study of how stress reshapes the brain, and his later work showed that chronic stress can cost hippocampal neurons. In 1993, with Eliot Stellar ("Stress and the individual: mechanisms leading to disease"), he introduced *allostatic load*. It built on *allostasis* (Sterling and Eyer, 1988), the idea that the body stays stable by changing and anticipating demands. His 1998 *New England Journal of Medicine* review was "Protective and damaging effects of stress mediators". His book for general readers was *The End of Stress as We Know It*. No public rupture appears in the sources checked. His turning points were scientific.

**How he thinks.**
- *Same mediators, two faces* (paraphrase of the 1998 argument): stress hormones and immune signals protect in the short run and cause wear when overused or slow to switch off.
- *Allostatic load* (paraphrase of 1993): the cost of chronic exposure to fluctuating or heightened neural and hormonal responses to repeated challenge.
- *Four patterns* (1998): repeated hits from many stressors; failure to adapt to the same stressor; a response that does not shut off; and a response too weak, so other systems overcompensate.
- *Measure across systems*: Seeman, McEwen and colleagues (1997, MacArthur Studies of Successful Aging) built an index from ten markers: cortisol, adrenaline, noradrenaline, DHEA-S, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, waist-hip ratio, total and HDL cholesterol, and HbA1c.

**Quality bar.**
1. Mechanism named: which mediator, in which body system?
2. Measured, not inferred: what number, from what input?
3. Both faces: the work admits the stress response protects as well as harms.
4. Time counted: load is cumulative, so the claim says over what period.
5. Honest index: there is no gold-standard measure, so say which markers were used.

**Lens questions.**
- What is measured here, with what instrument, and what would a wrong reading look like?
- Is this the moment's response (allostasis) or the long-term cost (load), and are they kept apart?
- Which of the four patterns is being described?
- Could someone else reproduce this number from the same inputs?
- Is the protective side of stress acknowledged?

**Applied to Lance's work.**
- Atüned: any load, resistance or CQ number on screen should name its inputs where the person can see them. McEwen's own field never fixed a single standard index. A number with no stated inputs fails this lens and the repository's rule 6.
- Book: chapters on stored stress should separate the two things McEwen kept apart, the moment's response and the accumulated cost.
- Book: citing allostatic load must not imply McEwen measured stress at a body address. His markers came from blood, urine, blood pressure and body shape.

**Bridge to Lance's canon.** Lance equates distortion with allostatic load. Overlap: stress leaves a real physical cost that accumulates and can outweigh any single event. Load is wear, which fits a mechanical model. Differences: allostatic load is systemic and measured across organ systems. McEwen did not place it at specific body locations, and nothing found in this research shows reading lowering an allostatic load index. Using the term is a borrowing. A claim that reading reduces it would need its own measurement.

- How to measure allostatic load is unsettled. Researchers use whatever markers they have, and Seeman's group later said the original ten were never meant to be a fixed standard. Results across studies are hard to compare.
- The four patterns come from a published reproduction of the 1998 figure; the article's full text was not read.
- The line "the brain is the central organ of stress" is widely tied to McEwen, but the exact wording was not confirmed, so it is not quoted here.
- Not re-confirmed this session: the journal of the 1993 paper (*Archives of Internal Medicine*), the book's year (2002) and co-author (Elizabeth Norton Lasley).
- Whether Seeman 1997 itself showed a link to mortality was not confirmed. Later cohorts test that link.

## 4. Peter A. Levine

**Who and why.** Founder of Somatic Experiencing (SE), a body-based trauma therapy built from watching how animals recover from threat. He is the lens for dose, pace and what a nervous system can hold.

**Background and history.** His official bio lists doctorates in biophysics (UC Berkeley) and psychology (International University), and work as a stress consultant to NASA on the early space shuttle programme. His institute's own account dates the method's start to 1969: during a session with a client, Nancy, who had panic and physical symptoms, he invoked the image of a tiger. The site reports that her panic attacks, agoraphobia and migraines dissolved after that one session. That is the developer's report, not an independent finding. He began teaching therapists in the late 1980s and founded the Foundation for Human Enrichment in the 1990s, which became Somatic Experiencing International. He leads the Ergos Institute. Key books: *Waking the Tiger: Healing Trauma* (1997) and *In an Unspoken Voice* (2010). His cost has been the gap between wide clinical use and a thin controlled-trial record, which critics keep pointing to.

**How he thinks.**
- *From animals* (paraphrase): wild animals tend to recover quickly from life-threatening events. His model holds that human trauma follows survival responses that were never completed.
- *Titration* (paraphrase of the SE glossary): break activation into pieces small enough to integrate easily, and raise intensity gradually as tolerance grows.
- *Pendulation*: the natural rhythm between constriction and release, used in session to move between hard material and a resourced present.
- *Resourcing*: build safety first, from supports inside the body (a calm area) or outside it (a helpful person, a safe place).
- *SIBAM*: track five channels: Sensation, Image, Behaviour, Affect, Meaning.

**Quality bar.**
1. Dose: is the hard material in pieces small enough to integrate?
2. A way back: is there always a return to a resourced state?
3. Sensation first: is the body's present signal tracked, not just the story?
4. Settling over catharsis: the goal is completion and calm, not intensity.
5. Pace set by the person: the person's nervous system sets the speed, not the programme.

**Lens questions.**
- How much activation does this ask for at once, and who decides when it is enough?
- Where is the resource the person returns to between hard moments?
- Does it track sensation now, or chase a memory?
- What happens if the person is overwhelmed halfway through?
- Is intensity being mistaken for progress?

**Applied to Lance's work.**
- Atüned: a reading that targets a charged address should be dosed. Open one address, then return to a neutral or pleasant place in the body before the next. Stacking heavy addresses in one sitting fails titration.
- Atüned: a stop should be available at any point, and it should lead to a settling step, not a blank screen.
- Book: where the text promises a big release, this lens prefers small, repeatable ones. A chapter that ends at peak activation should end at settling.

**Bridge to Lance's canon.** Overlap: both hold that unresolved stress lives in the body and is released through the body, not by argument alone. Both treat release as a physical event. Differences: SE is guided live by a trained practitioner tracking sensation, while Lance's release happens through reading, often alone. Levine's model is about incomplete defensive responses, not stress at fixed addresses. "Inflammation" is not his term. His physiology leans partly on polyvagal theory, which is under serious scientific challenge (see below). The animal-discharge story is a clinical model, not established biology.

The 2021 scoping review (16 studies) found preliminary positive effects but mixed study quality. In its words, the results "require more support from unbiased RCT-research."

- *In an Unspoken Voice* draws on Porges's polyvagal theory. Grossman (2023, *Biological Psychology*) argues its five basic premises are untenable or highly implausible. Porges disputes this.
- Reach figures on his own sites conflict (30,000 practitioners on one page, far more on another). Do not repeat them.
- Not re-confirmed: his birth year and the publication year of *Trauma and Memory*.

## 5. Jiddu Krishnamurti (1895-1986)

**Who and why.** Indian teacher raised to be a world messiah who gave the role up. He is the lens for authority, dependence and whether the work hands the person back to themselves.

**Background and history.** Born May 11, 1895, in Madanapalle, India. In 1909 (one source says 1910) the Theosophist C. W. Leadbeater picked him out on the beach at the Theosophical Society's headquarters in Adyar as the vehicle for a coming World Teacher. In 1911 the Society founded the Order of the Star in the East with him at its head. His younger brother Nitya, his closest companion, died of tuberculosis in 1925. On August 3, 1929, at the Star Camp in Ommen, Holland, before about 3,000 members, he dissolved the Order: "Truth is a pathless land, and you cannot approach it by any path whatsoever, by any religion, by any sect." It cost him the organisation and the role he had been raised for since boyhood. He spoke and wrote in public for more than fifty years afterward and died in Ojai, California, on February 17, 1986. Contested later: Radha Rajagopal Sloss's *Lives in the Shadow with J. Krishnamurti* (1991) disclosed a long affair with her mother, Rosalind Rajagopal. It also described bitter legal fights over money and property with her father, his long-time associate. Mary Lutyens replied in *Krishnamurti and the Rajagopals*.

**How he thinks.**
- *No authority*: truth, "being limitless, unconditioned, unapproachable by any path whatsoever, cannot be organized" (1929 speech). No teacher, method or organisation can bring it to you.
- *Choiceless awareness* (paraphrase of Foundation material): watching what is, without choosing, judging or trying to change it. It is also the title of a volume of his Collected Works (1948-1949).
- *The observer is the observed* (paraphrase): the one who watches anger is not separate from the anger. Seeing this, through careful attention, ends the inner split between controller and controlled. It is also a Collected Works title (1945-1948).
- *Observation over analysis*: "Observation is the total denial of analysis" is the title of a passage on the Foundation's site.

**Quality bar.**
1. No borrowed authority: does it rest on the reader's own seeing, or on a name?
2. Less dependence over time: does the person need the work less as they go?
3. Seeing before naming: the experience is observed before it is labelled.
4. No promised arrival: it does not sell a path to a fixed state.
5. No comparison: it does not measure the person against others or an ideal self.

**Lens questions.**
- Who is the authority here: the reader, Lance, the formula or the app?
- Does it ask the person to look, or tell them what they will find?
- Is a label (address, distortion, score) standing in for direct observation?
- Does it make followers, or people who no longer need it?
- What happens to the person's own seeing when the number disagrees with it?

**Applied to Lance's work.**
- Atüned: Lance's rule, "you don't need a teacher; you are the teacher", is this lens. On any screen that tells a user what they feel, ask what they notice first, then show the map.
- Book: the CQ formula is a "path" in Krishnamurti's sense. This lens does not say drop it. It says present it as a map to check against experience, not a truth to accept.
- Atüned: if the app uses streaks, levels or comparison with others, this lens fails them. They turn looking into becoming.

**Bridge to Lance's canon.** Overlap: Lance's refusal to be the teacher is close to the 1929 speech. Both put the person's own seeing first. Differences: Krishnamurti distrusted systems, methods and any measuring of inner life, and Atüned is a system with a method and a formula. CQ puts Intention in the numerator, while choiceless awareness is attention without choosing or striving. He did not speak of stress at body addresses. He is the lens most likely to disagree with the product, which is exactly why he is useful.

## Better candidates?

Lance said that if there are better people, he wants them. Three to consider. Lance decides.

**Lisa Feldman Barrett (neuroscience, emotion).** Arguably a stronger science lens than McEwen for Lance's purposes, because she links allostasis to felt experience.
- Evidence: Kleckner, Barrett and colleagues (*Nature Human Behaviour*, 2017) proposed a large-scale brain system for allostasis and interoception (sensing the body from inside), using two resting-scan samples of 280 and 270 people. In *How Emotions Are Made* (2017) she argues the brain builds emotions as predictions from bodily signals, and describes the body's resources as a "body budget".
- Controversy: her constructed-emotion theory is disputed by basic-emotion researchers, and a commentator (Neuroskeptic) questioned how much of the 2017 paper is evidence and how much speculation. She would also challenge fixed body "addresses", which is useful friction.
- Sources: https://www.affective-science.org/pubs/2017/kleckner-et-al-nature-2017.pdf, https://www.discovermagazine.com/mind/is-allostasis-the-brains-essential-function, https://nautil.us/how-emotions-connect-your-body-and-brain-237725

**Antonio Damasio (neurology, feeling and the body).**
- Evidence: his somatic marker hypothesis (popularised in *Descartes' Error*) holds that body signals tagged to past outcomes guide decisions. It was tested with the Iowa Gambling Task, which he co-created with Bechara, Hanna Damasio and Anderson.
- Controversy: Dunn, Dalgleish and Lawrence (2006) found inconsistent evidence and concluded the hypothesis "requires additional empirical support to remain tenable." The Iowa task's design and interpretation are also criticised.
- Sources: https://www.mrc-cbu.cam.ac.uk/personal/tim.dalgleish/dunnsmhreview.pdf, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iowa_gambling_task

**Ramana Maharshi (contemplative, 1879-1950).** A cleaner contemplative lens than Krishnamurti, with no organisational scandal.
- Evidence: *Who Am I?* (*Nan Yar*), his answers to Sivaprakasam Pillai around 1901-1902 at Arunachala, first published in Tamil in 1923. It is a classic of self-inquiry. He lived and taught at Tiruvannamalai until his death in 1950.
- Controversy: little about the man. The tension is in the teaching: self-inquiry sets aside the body and mind as "not I" (paraphrase), which sits awkwardly with a somatic map. Sources differ on the edition (13 or 28 questions) and the year (1901 or 1902).
- Sources: https://www.gururamana.org/Resources/Books/Who_Am_I_English.pdf, https://archive.arunachala.org/docs/who-ai

**Considered, not recommended over these.** Stephen Porges: polyvagal theory is widely used in somatic work, but Grossman (2023, *Biological Psychology*) argues all five of its basic premises are untenable or highly implausible, and Porges disputes that (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37230290/, https://polyvagalinstitute.org/vagal-paradox). For a precision-based product that is too much contested ground to lean on. Bessel van der Kolk was not researched in this pass.
