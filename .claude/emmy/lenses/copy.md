# Copy for Emmy: five copy editors and five copywriters, as lenses for Atüned and The Mechanics of Being
Researched 2026-10-15.
Lenses, not authorities: Emmy reviews through them, she never speaks for them.

---

**How to read this.** This environment blocked direct page fetches (Wikipedia, NPR, TED and the
publisher sites all refused), so every fact rests on source text as web search returned it, from
the source named beside it. Full pages and books were not read. Quotes appear only where the exact
words came back with a URL, and that URL is in the section's sources. Everything else is
paraphrase. **UNVERIFIED** marks anything that could not be confirmed. Example notes use real
Atüned strings from `atuned/src/` as they stood on 2026-10-15. They are suggestions for Lance,
never rulings, and Atüned's `docs/VOICE.md` governs wherever a lens disagrees with it.

**How Emmy uses a lens.** Pick the lens that fits the job, run its tests, and write the note as
"Through the Dreyer lens: ...". Never "Dreyer would say". Lance decides.

# PART 1: COPY EDITORS

## 1. Benjamin Dreyer

**Who and why.** Random House's longtime copy chief. The lens for cutting reflexive words and for
telling a real rule from a superstition.

**Background.** He worked as a freelance proofreader, then copy edited at St. Martin's Press, then
moved to Random House, where he became vice president, executive managing editor and copy chief.
NPR marked his retirement in December 2023 after three decades there. *Dreyer's English: An
Utterly Correct Guide to Clarity and Style* (Random House, January 29, 2019) became a New York
Times bestseller.

**How he thinks.** The copy editor comes after the writer and the editor, and the job is to
burnish what is there. A secondary report has him saying he can make a good piece better but
cannot make a bad piece good. The book opens with a challenge: go a week without writing very,
rather, really, quite and in fact. He adds just, so, pretty, of course, surely, that said and
actually as words he is happy to lose. On NPR he called the first group "wan intensifiers" and
offered "brilliant" in place of "very smart": one stronger word instead of a prop. He is not
absolutist. "Very" is a pet word to do without where possible, not a crime. The book separates
the rules of grammar from the non-rules, and NPR's headline caught his stance: stand tall, but
choose your battles.

**Quality bar.**
1. Strike every wan intensifier and throat-clearer. If the sentence loses nothing, they stay out.
2. Where an intensifier propped a weak word, one stronger word replaces both.
3. Every correction rests on a real rule, not a remembered superstition.
4. The edited line is still the author's sentence, burnished, not replaced.

**Lens questions.**
- Which words here are habit, not meaning?
- Is this intensifier doing work, or covering for a word that is not there yet?
- Is this fix a rule or a preference? Say which.
- Is this battle worth the author's attention?

**Example notes.**
- `43_what-surfaced.js`, Controlled Breath: "When the breaths are really slow a great deal of
  healing happens." "Really" is a wan intensifier, and the card already gave the number (two to
  six breaths a minute). "At that pace ..." lets the number do the intensifying. "A great deal of
  healing" is a Hopkins problem too (see 9).
- `43_what-surfaced.js`: "No release run yet, so nothing has been added to today from the
  imprints." The "so" is throat-clearing between two facts. Two sentences read cleaner: "No
  release run yet. Nothing from the imprints has been added to today."
- Book: "Healing is the body learning that presence is no longer dangerous." No intensifier, no
  throat-clearing. "No longer" is load-bearing: it says presence once was dangerous. Pass.

**Sources.**

- https://www.npr.org/2023/12/09/1218386146/benjamin-dreyer-copy-editor-and-author-of-dreyers-english-retires-from-random-ho
- https://themillions.com/?p=108666
- https://villanovan.com/4181/uncategorized/new-york-times-best-selling-author-benjamin-dreyer-visits-the-university/
- https://twi-ny.com/2019/01/29/twi-ny-talk-benjamin-dreyer-dreyers-english/

**Uncertain.** The year he joined Random House and the year he became copy chief: UNVERIFIED (1993
is often given; not confirmed). The punctuation of the opening challenge differs between editions.
"Good piece better, bad piece good" is paraphrase from a secondary report.

**Better candidates?** William Zinsser, as a cutting lens, though he was a writer and teacher, not
a copy editor. *On Writing Well* (Harper & Row, 1976) opens on "Clutter is the disease of American
writing" (https://www.bangordailynews.com/2015/05/14/news/william-zinsser-who-wrote-the-popular-guide-on-writing-well-dies-at-92/),
and his method is subtraction, which is Lance's: "The work is not adding. It is removing."

## 2. Mary Norris

**Who and why.** The New Yorker's copy editor and query proofreader. The lens for consistent house
style and for punctuation that tells the reader what matters.

**Background.** She joined The New Yorker in 1978 and worked in its copy department for more than
three decades. One source dates her query-proofreader role to 1993. *Between You & Me: Confessions
of a Comma Queen* (W. W. Norton, April 2015) grew out of her grammar posts for the magazine's
website and was a finalist for the 2016 Thurber Prize for American Humor. Her TED2016 talk, "The
nit-picking glory of The New Yorker's Comma Queen," is described as showing that her work aims to
make authors look good. *Greek to Me* followed in 2019.

**How she thinks.** Style is a promise of consistency. In a 2015 interview with Carol Fisher
Saller she compared a house style to a favourite restaurant: you know what to expect and you are
not let down. Writers should accommodate their publisher's style, so for her own book she used
Norton's, not The New Yorker's. Her closing advice on style was not to overthink it. She defends
the magazine's "close" punctuation, commas that fence off material not essential to the sentence,
because they show the reader what the sentence can do without. She records the magazine's
grammarian, Eleanor Gould, telling her to leave a tangled possessive alone: knowing when not to
touch is part of the job. Reviewers call her blunt but never snooty, and she says she avoids
correcting people in public.

**Quality bar.**
1. The same thing is styled the same way everywhere: capitals, numerals, hyphens, fixed nouns.
2. Every comma marks a real boundary between essential and not essential.
3. Every hyphen settles a reading.
4. After the edit the author looks better, not more edited.
5. When a fix would make the sentence worse, leave it alone.

**Lens questions.**
- Is this term capitalised, spelled and hyphenated as it is on every other screen?
- Does this comma tell the reader that what it fences off is optional? Is it?
- What does the reader expect here from the last ten screens?
- Would fixing this make it worse?

**Example notes.**
- `43_what-surfaced.js`: "The first practice in the Body Tract." "Body Tract" appears once in the
  source, and every practice card is tagged by track (`track:'Body'`, `'Somatic'`, `'Energy'`,
  `'Mind'`). Query, do not change: is "Tract" meant, or is it "Body Track"?
- `20_markup.html`: "Offline. Your work is saved on this device and will not sync until you are
  back." "Back" is loose: back where? "Until you are online again" answers the line's first word.
- Book: "Healing is the body learning that presence is no longer dangerous." The "that" clause is
  essential, so no comma. Correct as written.

**Sources.**
- https://cmosshoptalk.com/2015/04/29/mary-norris-the-new-yorkers-comma-queen-talks-about-styles-tolerance-and-her-new-book/
- https://www.npr.org/transcripts/397094248
- https://www.ted.com/talks/mary_norris_the_nit_picking_glory_of_the_new_yorker_s_comma_queen
- https://www.bookreporter.com/reviews/between-you-and-me-confessions-of-a-comma-queen
- https://vineyardgazette.com/arts-entertainment/books-ideas/2015/07/30/let-us-now-praise-humble-apostrophe/
- https://www.wheelercentre.com/events-tickets/past-seasons/past-season-2019/mary-norris-from-comma-queen-to-greek-geek

**Uncertain.** Query proofreader from 1993: single source. The subtitle of *Greek to Me* varies
between "a" and "the" Comma Queen. Whether she has since retired: not checked. "We are not the
enemy," a line often attached to her, could not be found: UNVERIFIED, do not use. The TED talk was
known from its description only.

**Better candidates?** None.

## 3. Carol Fisher Saller

**Who and why.** The Chicago Manual of Style's Q&A editor. The lens for editing without damage and
for asking the writer a good question.

**Background.** She was a senior manuscript editor at the University of Chicago Press and the
editor of the Chicago Manual of Style Online Q&A. A 2009 press release said she read every one of
the thousands of questions sent in each year. *The Subversive Copy Editor: Advice from Chicago (or,
How to Negotiate Good Relationships with Your Writers, Your Colleagues, and Yourself)* came out in
2009. The second edition (2016) added chapters on outdated grammar and style rules and on keeping
current. She wrote the Editor's Corner column on CMOS Shop Talk and is now retired.

**How she thinks.** Carefulness, transparency and flexibility build trust, and the author is a
collaborator, not an enemy. The book starts from one observation: when writer and editor square
off, it is often the reader who loses. "Subversive" means throwing out inflexible rules that get in
the way, and breaking a rule when that serves the reader. In a 2015 column she calls "do no harm"
the first rule of copyediting. The overeager editor is the one most likely to introduce errors,
and the worst disasters are errors the editor creates. Every change should rest on an authority you
could cite if asked, without writing it in the margin. Look it up before you query. A 2017 column
gives the check before enforcing a style point: does it help the reader, was the writer really
inconsistent, and is the change worth its time and its risk of a new error?

**Quality bar.**
1. Every change has an authority you can name. For Atüned: VOICE.md, the product bible, a decision
   number.
2. The change helps the reader, not the editor's taste.
3. The edit introduced no new error and kept the meaning.
4. A query is specific, answerable and polite, with the authority attached.

**Lens questions.**
- What is my authority for this change?
- Who loses if I leave it? Who loses if I change it?
- Did my edit change what Lance meant?
- Is this a query or a correction?

**Example notes.**
- `20_markup.html`, the story screen lead: "Everything you are holding." Authority: VOICE.md rule
  3 lists "holding" as banned wellness language. A query, not a correction: "VOICE.md rule 3 bans
  'holding.' Keep it as a deliberate exception, or change it? One option from VOICE.md's own
  table: 'Everything in your own words.'"
- `43_what-surfaced.js`, The Somatic Truth Check: "Expansion is true. Contraction is distortion."
  No authority requires a change. Leave it, unless Lance wants the pair made parallel (see Garner).
- Book: "Healing is the body learning that presence is no longer dangerous." No authority would
  justify touching it. Do no harm.

**Sources.**
- https://cmosshoptalk.com/2015/11/30/editors-corner-4-2/ ("do no harm")
- https://cmosshoptalk.com/2017/01/31/3-rookie-editing-habits-you-can-stop-making-right-now/
- https://pressblog.uchicago.edu/2009/03/17/press-release-saller-the-subversive-copy-editor.html
- https://www.bibliovault.org/BV.book.epl?ISBN=9780226240077
- https://muse.jhu.edu/article/383568

**Uncertain.** Her years at the Press and the years she ran the Q&A were not found. The book itself
was not read: its principles come from publisher copy, reviews and her own columns.

**Better candidates?** None.

## 4. Bryan A. Garner

**Who and why.** Lexicographer and usage authority. The lens for the exact word, judged on
evidence of how the language is used.

**Background.** Born in 1958 in Lubbock, Texas, and educated at the University of Texas at Austin,
he is a lawyer, grammarian and lexicographer. He is president of LawProse, editor in chief of
*Black's Law Dictionary*, and author of the "Grammar and Usage" chapter of *The Chicago Manual of
Style*. His usage dictionary began as *A Dictionary of Modern American Usage* (1998; its 25th
anniversary was marked in 2024) and is now *Garner's Modern English Usage* (4th ed. 2016; 5th ed.
Oxford University Press, 2022, with usage checked against Google Ngram data). With Justice Antonin
Scalia he wrote *Making Your Case* (2008) and *Reading Law* (2012). In 2009 the Burton Awards named
him Legal-Writing and Reference-Book Author of the Decade. David Foster Wallace's long review of
the first edition, "Tense Present," carried his name outside the law.

**How he thinks.** He calls himself a prescriptivist who does not shy away from judgments, and
also a "descriptive prescriber": the judgment rests on how the language is actually used. His tool
is the Language-Change Index, which places each disputed usage on five stages: rejected, widely
shunned, widespread but..., ubiquitous but..., fully accepted. His essay "Making Peace in the
Language Wars" proposes a truce between the camps. Critics such as Language Hat argue he is more
prescriptivist than the truce suggests.

**Quality bar.**
1. Each disputed usage is placed on the five stages before it is defended or changed.
2. Paired sentences are grammatically parallel, not only rhythmically.
3. The exact word, not its neighbour.
4. A judgment is stated plainly, with its evidence, not hedged.

**Lens questions.**
- Where on the five stages does this usage sit, and who will notice?
- Is this the exact word, or the one next to it?
- Do the paired sentences match in grammar as well as rhythm?
- Is a defined term used in exactly its defined sense?

**Example notes.**
- `43_what-surfaced.js`: "Expansion is true. Contraction is distortion." Not parallel: an
  adjective, then a noun. Either "Expansion is true. Contraction is false." or "Expansion is
  signal. Contraction is distortion." The two say different things. Lance picks the meaning.
- `43_what-surfaced.js`: "Where you feel them now is where they are displaced to." "Displaced to"
  reads stiff. "Where you feel them now is where they have moved." This is Emmy's usage judgment,
  not a Garner entry: check the dictionary before calling it more than taste.
- Book: "Healing is the body learning that presence is no longer dangerous." "Presence" carries
  the sentence. If it is a defined term in the book, hold it to exactly that sense everywhere, the
  way VOICE.md fixes the layer nouns.

**Sources.**
- https://lawprose.org/bryan-garner/
- https://lawprose.org/?p=52 (Language-Change Index)
- https://global.oup.com/academic/product/garners-modern-english-usage-9780197599020
- https://linguistlist.org/issues/34/3356/
- https://www.vocabulary.com/articles/dogeared/a-descriptive-prescribers-useful-usage-guide/
- https://editorstorontoblog.com/2024/01/18/from-mondegreens-to-portmanteaus-a-celebration-of-the-25th-anniversary-of-garners-modern-english-usage/
- https://languagehat.com/the-language-wars/
- https://chicagoreference.com/ucp/books/author/G/B/au5485404.html

**Uncertain.** The issue and year of Wallace's "Tense Present" in Harper's: UNVERIFIED (often
given as 2001). The year he became editor of *Black's*: not confirmed. *Legal Writing in Plain
English*, often listed among his books, did not come up in search: UNVERIFIED.

**Better candidates?** None.

## 5. Gordon Lish

**Who and why.** A line editor, not a copy editor. The lens for the hard cut, and the standing
warning about what a cut can take.

**The difference.** A copy editor corrects, makes consistent and protects the author's intent. A
line editor reshapes the prose itself: sentences, order, endings. Lish is the far end of it.

**Background.** Fiction editor at *Esquire* 1969 to 1976, editor at Knopf 1977 to 1995, and a
teacher of writers including Barry Hannah and Gary Lutz ("Captain Fiction," in Amy Hempel's
essay). He edited Raymond Carver's *What We Talk About When We Talk About Love*
(Knopf, 1981). D.T. Max's "The Carver Chronicles" (*New York Times Magazine*, August 9, 1998),
drawn from the Lish papers at Indiana University's Lilly Library, found he had cut most stories by
half or more, rewritten endings, retitled stories and renamed characters. Carver wrote to Lish in
1980 trying to stop the book in that form, then wrote a week later that he was thrilled with it.
After Carver's death, Tess Gallagher pushed for the original, and the Library of America printed it
as *Beginners* in Carver's *Collected Stories* (2009). Critics split on whether the originals show
Lish's genius or his overreach. In a 2015 *Paris Review* interview, "The Art of Editing No. 2," he
defended the work.

**How he thinks.** From his teaching, as students report it: the first sentence is the "attack
sentence"; each next sentence is built from what the last one implies ("consecution"); a "swerve"
breaks the predictable. Hempel recalls him skipping a page and a half of a student's preamble to
find where the story began.

**Quality bar.**
1. The first sentence attacks. Anything before it is preamble.
2. Each sentence grows out of the one before.
3. Cut half. What survives is the piece.
4. The Carver test: after the cut, is it still the author's voice, and did the author agree?

**Lens questions.**
- Where does this actually start?
- What does this sentence owe the last one?
- What happens if this paragraph goes?
- Whose voice is this now?

**Example notes.**
- Book: "Healing is the body learning that presence is no longer dangerous." An attack sentence.
  If it sits mid-chapter, try it as the chapter's first line.
- `43_what-surfaced.js`: "No release run yet, so nothing has been added to today from the
  imprints." Cut test: "No release yet. Nothing from the imprints today." First check that
  "release run" is not a fixed term before dropping "run."
- On Emmy's own edits: Lance's edit is a cut (voice profile, pattern 14). This lens is also the
  brake: no cut that changes what he meant ships without his sign-off.

**Sources.**

- https://ijas.iaas.ie/the-poetics-of-the-sentence-examining-gordon-lishs-literary-legacy/
- https://www.pifmagazine.com/2000/11/lish-gordonnotes-and-reflections-of-a-former-student/
- https://www.theparisreview.org/interviews/6423/the-art-of-editing-no-2-gordon-lish
- https://donswaim.com/nytimes.carverchronicles.html (Max, 1998, reproduced)
- https://www.webdelsol.com/LITARTS/In_Dissent/cooper3.htm
- https://www.npr.org/transcripts/17910720
- https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/turn-every-page-film-review-2022

**Uncertain.** How much was cut: sources give about half overall and 70 to 78 percent for some
stories, so say "about half" unless a single story is checked. The wording of Lish's 2015 defence,
reported as "Had I not revised Carver, would he be paid the attention given him? Baloney!", was not
checked against the interview text: UNVERIFIED. The date of the 2007 *New Yorker* printing of
Carver's original and his letters: UNVERIFIED. His teaching terms come from students and critics,
not a method he wrote down.

**Better candidates?** Robert Gottlieb, a line editor without the overreach: he edited *Catch-22*
and worked with Robert Caro for about fifty years (*Turn Every Page*, 2022; memoir *Avid Reader*,
FSG, 2016). Reviewers report his view that the worst thing an editor can do is try to turn a book
into something other than a better version of itself (wording UNVERIFIED).

## 6. David Ogilvy

**Who and why.** Founder of Ogilvy & Mather. The lens for the headline that carries the message,
the fact over the adjective, and writing the way you talk.

**Background.** He sold Aga cookers and in 1935 wrote the company's sales manual, "The Theory and
Practice of Selling the Aga Cooker," which *Fortune* later called probably the best sales manual
ever written. From 1938 he worked at George Gallup's Audience Research Institute, which grounded
his faith in research. *Confessions of an Advertising Man* (Atheneum, 1963) sold more than a
million copies, and *Ogilvy on Advertising* followed in 1983. His 1958 Rolls-Royce ad, whose
headline says the loudest noise at 60 miles an hour is the electric clock, is the one he called
his best headline. It came out of weeks of reading technical material on the car, and one account
says the line was a motoring journalist's observation that he lifted.

**How he thinks.** Research first, then one specific fact the reader can check. The headline does
most of the work: he wrote that far more people read the headline than the body (the usual figure
is five to one, which he credited loosely to research and which critics now call dated). His 1982
memo "How to Write," sent to all staff, gives ten hints. Among them: read Roman and Raphaelson's
*Writing That Works*; write the way you talk; use short words, sentences and paragraphs; never use
jargon (he names "reconceptualize" and "demassification"); keep memos to two pages; read the draft
aloud the next morning, then edit it. He said nobody should touch advertising before reading
Hopkins's *Scientific Advertising* seven times (wording varies).

**Quality bar.**
1. The headline alone delivers the point.
2. One specific, checkable fact beats any adjective.
3. It reads like talk: short words, no jargon.
4. It survives being read aloud the next morning.
5. It treats the reader as intelligent.

**Lens questions.**
- If they read only this line, do they get it?
- What is the one fact that proves it?
- Which word is jargon to someone outside the app?
- Read aloud, where do you stumble?
- What should the reader do next?

**Example notes.**
- `43_what-surfaced.js`, lock error: "The lock did not come off." Plain and true, but VOICE.md rule
  6 says an error says what happened and what to do. This says only the first half. Add the real
  next step.
- `20_markup.html`: "First person. Say it the way you would say it." This is "write the way you
  talk," pointed at the user. Pass.
- Book: "Healing is the body learning that presence is no longer dangerous." Works as a headline:
  one line, one claim. The paragraph under it owes the fact: what changes in the body, and how the
  reader can check it in their own.

**Sources.**
- https://www.fs.blog/2013/06/david-ogilvy-10-tips-on-writing/ (memo, reproduced)
- https://brajeshwar.com/2024/how-to-write-by-david-ogilvy/
- https://enchantingmarketing.com/david-ogilvy-rolls-royce-advert
- https://flintoff.org/he-was-never-modest
- https://www.london.edu/think/business-heroes-david-ogilvy-the-father-of-modern-advertising
- https://review.brunswickgroup.com/article/the-wizard-of-ads/
- https://qz.com/1561802
- https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-david-ogilvy/
- https://draytonbird.com/?p=2365 (Ogilvy on Hopkins)

**Uncertain.** "The consumer isn't a moron; she is your wife": wording and location UNVERIFIED, use
only as paraphrase (treat the reader as intelligent). "When you have written your headline, you
have spent eighty cents out of your dollar": found only on a secondary site that places it in
*Confessions*, UNVERIFIED. Most copies date the memo September 7, 1982; one says 1981. Who first
wrote the Rolls-Royce line is contested. "I do not regard advertising as entertainment or an art
form" did not come up: do not use.

**Better candidates?** None.

## 7. Julian Koenig

**Who and why.** The DDB copywriter behind Volkswagen's "Think Small" and "Lemon." The lens for
honest admission as the strongest proof.

**Background.** Koenig (1921 to June 12, 2014) wrote at Doyle Dane Bernbach with art director
Helmut Krone: "Think Small" (1959, the Beetle's first campaign) and "Lemon" (1960). Volkswagen's
1960 US budget was about $800,000, which pushed DDB toward plain black-and-white pages. He wrote
the Timex line usually given as "Takes a licking and keeps on ticking," co-founded Papert Koenig
Lois in 1960 with Fred Papert and George Lois, and entered the One Club Creative Hall of Fame in
1966. He named Earth Day, which fell on his birthday, choosing the rhyme over "Ecology Day" and "E
Day." Credit was a lifelong fight: George Lois claimed "Think Small," and The New York Times ran a
correction saying he did not create it. Late in life Koenig called advertising built on puffery
and, at heart, deception, and in a 2009 *This American Life* interview doubted anyone could go
proudly into the next world on such a career (both paraphrased). The Drum headlined his own
summary: "I just wrote some ads."

**How he thinks.** He left no method book, so this is read from the work: Emmy's reading, not his
statement. "Lemon" calls the product a lemon in the headline, then proves it with one small defect:
a blemished chrome strip on the glove compartment, caught by an inspector named Kurt Kroner. It
closes by turning the admission into the benefit: "We pluck the lemons; you get the plums." "Think
Small" makes the car's smallness the point instead of hiding it. The headlines are one or two
words; the picture and the plain body do the rest.

**Quality bar.**
1. It says the uncomfortable true thing first.
2. One concrete detail, with a name or a number, proves it.
3. The headline is as short as it can be and still land.
4. The close turns the admission into the reason to trust.

**Lens questions.**
- What limit are we not saying?
- What single detail proves we are telling the truth?
- Can the headline be two words?
- Would the person who wrote this be proud of it in ten years?

**Example notes.**
- `43_what-surfaced.js`: "This is training, not release." It admits what the practice is not.
  Keep.
- `43_what-surfaced.js`, The Signal Test: "Proof that a word with no quality of its own moves your
  body." "Proof" is the biggest claim on the screen. It holds only if the test shows the reader the
  measured movement. If it does, show the number (CLAUDE.md rule 6). If not, "A test of whether ..."
  is the honest version.
- Book: "Healing is the body learning that presence is no longer dangerous." Where is the
  admission? A next sentence on what this costs, or how long it takes, makes the promise
  believable.

## 8. Dan Wieden

**Who and why.** Co-founder of Wieden+Kennedy and writer of Nike's "Just Do It." The lens for the
one line that holds every piece together.

**Background.** Wieden (1945 to September 30, 2022) founded Wieden+Kennedy with David Kennedy in
Portland, Oregon, on April Fool's Day 1982, with one client: Nike. "Just Do It" ran from 1988. He
told its origin himself, in the 2009 documentary *Art & Copy* and at Design Indaba in 2015
(reported by Dezeen): it came from the last words of Gary Gilmore, a murderer raised in Portland,
before his execution by firing squad: "Let's do it." Wieden did not like the "let's," so he changed
it, and joked that otherwise he would have had to give Gilmore credit. Phil Knight's first reaction
was that Nike did not need it; Wieden asked him to trust it. Wieden put the agency into a trust to
keep it independent and stepped back from daily operations in 2015.

**How he thinks.** The line was written to solve a structural problem: Nike had a set of very
different commercials that did not add up to anything without a tagline. The tagline is the spine,
not a slogan. In *Art & Copy* he said the real risk lies in trying to be authentic (paraphrase). He
was open about where the line came from, even though the source was dark.

**Quality bar.**
1. One line that every screen, ad and page could sit under.
2. Short, imperative, in the reader's mouth rather than the brand's.
3. Authentic: it sounds like the people who make the thing.
4. The source of the line is known and can be told.

**Lens questions.**
- What one line makes these screens one product?
- Would a user say this line to themselves?
- Is the attitude ours, or borrowed?
- Where did this line come from, and would we say so?

**Example notes.**
- Atüned as a whole: the practices, release, reframe and ritual each speak well alone. What single
  line do they all sit under? Lance's own words already hold candidates ("The work is not adding.
  It is removing." and "No one is coming to save you."). Emmy can propose; Lance picks.
- `43_what-surfaced.js`, Feel the Resistance: "Direct contact dissolves charge. No analysis, no
  escape." Spine-grade: short, close to an imperative, and it sounds like Lance.
- Book: "Healing is the body learning that presence is no longer dangerous." A thesis, not a
  tagline: it explains rather than moves. Keep it in the text; do not make it the brand line.

## 9. Claude C. Hopkins

**Who and why.** The Lord & Thomas copywriter who wrote *Scientific Advertising*. The lens for
specific claims, counted results, and telling the process nobody else tells.

**Background.** Albert Lasker hired him to Lord & Thomas in 1907 or 1908, at 41, at a reported
$185,000 a year, and he rose to president and chairman. He ran test campaigns on keyed coupons and
free samples; one Van Camp's evaporated milk ad is said to have drawn more than 1.46 million
coupons. *Scientific Advertising* appeared in 1923 and *My Life in Advertising* (Harper & Brothers)
in 1927. He died in September 1932 in Spring Lake, Michigan.

**How he thinks.** Advertising is salesmanship, and its only purpose is to make sales (chapter 2,
"Just Salesmanship"; wording from a secondary reproduction). So every ad is a test: key it, count
the response, keep what sells. Be specific. From chapter 7, "Being Specific": "Platitudes and
generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck." His Schlitz example: every
brewer said "pure." He described the process, which every brewer used and none had told: bottles
washed four times by machinery, water from deep wells, filtered-air cooling rooms, 1,018 yeast
experiments. Telling the process first made it theirs.

**Quality bar.**
1. Every claim is specific and checkable, and every number has its unit.
2. The claim has been tested on real people and the result counted.
3. It tells the process others leave out.
4. It asks for one action.

**Lens questions.**
- What is the number, and what is its unit?
- How do we know this works? What was counted?
- Which generality here would roll off?
- What do we do that nobody bothers to say?
- What one thing does the reader do next?

**Example notes.**
- `43_what-surfaced.js`, Box Breathing: "Four and four, or eight and eight." Four what? The card's
  own detail says each of four phases (inhale, hold, exhale, hold) takes "the same count," so "four
  and four" reads as two phases where there are four. Name the unit and the shape, for example
  "Four counts each side. Eight when it is easy."
- `43_what-surfaced.js`, Controlled Breath: "Two to six breaths a minute" is Hopkins-grade. "Gets
  the energy below the heart," and below it "a great deal of healing happens," are generalities
  unless the app measures them. Under the real-numbers rule: show the measured change, or say it
  is the aim.
- Book: "Healing is the body learning that presence is no longer dangerous." The Schlitz move for
  the next sentence: lay out the mechanism step by step, the part other books in the category skip.

**Sources.**
- https://castlewoodstudios.com/downloads/scientific_advertising.pdf (full text; "water from a duck")
- https://www.holloway.com/g/scientific-advertising
- https://www.perlego.com/book/4696632/scientific-advertising-pdf (chapter list)
- https://bestwriting.com/?p=24641
- https://advertisinghalloffame.org/inductee/claude-clarence-hopkins/
- https://oncopy.substack.com/p/how-claude-hopkins-saved-schlitz
- https://fadeyev.net/scientific-advertising/
- https://draytonbird.com/?p=2365

**Uncertain.** The hire year (1907 or 1908) and the salary come from Ogilvy and later retellings.
The coupon count is single-source. The Schlitz result varies by source, from best-seller in six
months to a tie for first. Pepsodent's role in spreading toothbrushing is Charles Duhigg's
secondhand account. "Ads are not written to entertain" appears only on secondary sites: wording
UNVERIFIED.

**Better candidates?** John Caples, as an alternative rather than an upgrade. He wrote "They
Laughed When I Sat Down at the Piano" (1925 to 1926) and *Tested Advertising Methods* (1932); his
method is headline testing, so he overlaps Hopkins and adds the headline focus
(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Caples).

## 10. Eugene M. Schwartz

**Who and why.** Direct-mail copywriter and author of *Breakthrough Advertising*. The lens for
meeting readers where their awareness is, and for leading with the mechanism.

**Background.** Born in 1927 (one source: March 18, in Butte, Montana). One source has him as copy
chief at Huber Hoge & Sons before going freelance in 1954. He wrote mail-order copy for decades,
including early ads for Boardroom, and the books *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966; Boardroom
reissue 2004) and *The Brilliance Breakthrough*. With his wife Barbara he built one of the leading
collections of contemporary American art and gave hundreds of works to museums including the
Metropolitan Museum and MoMA; he also wrote *Confessions of a Poor Collector*. He died at his
Manhattan home in 1995, aged 68.

**How he thinks.** Desire comes from the market, not the copy: "Copy cannot create desire for a
product. It can only take the hopes, dreams, fears and desires that already exist in the hearts of
millions of people" (as reproduced; some copies read "exists"). The writer channels that desire
onto the product. Two scales: the reader's awareness (unaware, problem aware, solution aware,
product aware, most aware), which decides what the first line can assume; and the market's
sophistication, how many claims it has already heard. In a fresh market a plain claim works. As
claims pile up, a bigger promise stops working, and the copy has to show a new mechanism, then
elaborate it, and at last sell identification (stage wording varies across summaries). He is
reported to have written in timed bursts of 33 minutes 33 seconds.

**Quality bar.**
1. The opening meets the reader at their actual awareness level.
2. It channels a desire the reader already has, in words they already use.
3. In a crowded market, it names a mechanism instead of a bigger promise.
4. It never manufactures a need.

**Lens questions.**
- What does the reader already know when they reach this line?
- What do they already want, in their own words?
- How many times has this market heard this promise?
- What is our mechanism, stated plainly?

**Example notes.**
- Atüned sells into wellness, one of the most claim-saturated markets there is. Lance's own move,
  "Not a metaphor for it. The actual mechanism." (manuscript), is exactly the late-stage answer:
  lead with mechanism, not promise. Keep it central in marketing copy.
- `43_what-surfaced.js`: "What you absorbed from the people who raised you." Problem aware: the
  reader recognises it without needing its name. It channels a feeling they already have. Pass.
- Book: "Healing is the body learning that presence is no longer dangerous." For a solution-aware
  reader it lands. For an unaware reader "presence" is abstract. On a jacket or an opening page, put
  the felt problem first and let this line follow it.
