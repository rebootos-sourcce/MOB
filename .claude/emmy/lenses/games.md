# Emmy's lenses for game design (five designers) and video game art direction (five art directors), used to review Atüned and The Mechanics of Being
Researched 2026-10-15.
Lenses, not authorities: Emmy reviews through them, she never speaks for them.

**How this was researched.** Opening pages directly failed from this machine (DNS error), so each fact below was confirmed through web search results that quote or summarise the pages listed under each entry. No page was read in full. The few direct quotes come from search extracts, and each one gives the URL its words came from. Everything else is paraphrase. UNVERIFIED marks a claim that no result confirmed, or that only one result supported.

**How to use a lens.** A review note names the lens and the test that failed, then the fix: "Through Pajitnov: the loop does not hold with the numbers hidden. Fix the action before the summary." It never says what the person would think or feel.

**About the applied notes.** They refer to Atüned's daily release loop as the IA schematic describes it (PICK, ONBOARD, RELEASE with the ring opening, REFRAME with the ring closing, REST for 120 seconds, SUMMARY), to the ring around a mark, which is percent released and fills toward complete (bible 270), and to the coherence number at the ring's centre, which opens the Avatar (bible 598). Emmy has not checked how the coherence number is computed. Rule 6 (every number shown is real) is the house rule all of these lenses sit under.

## Part 1 · Game designers: mechanics, psychology, complex made simple, loops that stay fun

### Alexey Pajitnov

**Who and why.** The Soviet computer scientist who made Tetris. This is the lens for a loop that is complete with almost nothing in it.

**Background.** He worked at the Dorodnitsyn Computing Centre of the Soviet Academy of Sciences in Moscow, on speech recognition and artificial intelligence. Puzzles were his hobby, and pentominoes (shapes made of five squares) were a favourite from childhood. He built the first Tetris on an Electronika 60, a machine with no graphics, drawing the playing field with text characters. Henk Rogers and the Moscow Times give June 6, 1984 for the first playable version; Wikipedia says he finished it on June 6, 1985, after playtesting. He earned no royalties until the rights reverted in 1996, when he and Henk Rogers became partners in The Tetris Company. He later moved to Seattle and worked in Microsoft's games group (Hexic among others) until 2004 (official Tetris bio).

**How he thinks.** Three recorded decisions show the method. He cut the pieces from five squares to four because there are only seven four-square shapes, few enough to read and place in real time. He made full rows vanish because on that small text screen the well filled too fast: a fix for a constraint that became the heart of the game. His first prototype had no score and no levels, and he still could not stop playing it (his account in several interviews, paraphrased). He calls simplicity central and describes the game as taking the chaos of falling pieces and building something reasonable from it (Globe and Mail, paraphrased).

**How he innovates.** By subtraction under pressure. He took a puzzle he loved, removed a square from every piece, removed finished work from the screen, and added only time. The novelty came from a hardware limit and a childhood toy, not from added features. Even multiplayer resisted him for years: in 2010 he said the game's intensity leaves little attention for an opponent (Gamasutra, via Game Informer).

**Core loop and the psychology it uses.** A piece falls, you turn and place it, a full row clears, the next piece is already coming, the speed rises. It uses an open task that pulls toward completion, a random next piece so no two games repeat, and constant small decisions under time. Emmy's reading, not his: success disappears and mistakes stay in view, so the screen always shows exactly what is left to fix.

**Quality bar.**
1. With score, levels and decoration removed, the core action still holds a person for ten minutes.
2. Every element can be read in under a second, while moving.
3. Finished work leaves the screen; only open work stays.
4. One verb, one rule, one pressure. Nothing needs explaining after the first minute.

**Lens questions.**
- What is the falling piece here: the one thing that arrives and must be placed?
- If every number were hidden, would the loop still pull?
- What does the screen remove when the person succeeds?
- Which of our limits could become the core rule instead of a workaround?

**Example notes on Atüned.**
- Through Pajitnov: run the release loop with the summary numbers hidden. If stem, cascade, LEFT then RIGHT is not satisfying on its own, a better summary will not save it.
- Through Pajitnov: a full ring means done (bible 270). Does a done address then get out of the way? If cleared addresses keep equal weight on the body, the screen fills with finished work. Let full rings recede so only open work is loud.
- Through Pajitnov: the person picks a speed (2 to 10) and a count (10 to 100) before starting. Tetris asks one question: where does this piece go. Ask whether the picker can collapse to one choice, or to none on an ordinary day.

### Will Wright

**Who and why.** The designer of SimCity and The Sims. This is the lens for a simulation the person learns by poking it, and for the model the person builds in their head.

**Background.** Wright made Raid on Bungeling Bay (1984) and found the level editor more fun than the game, so he kept adding to it: cars, people, houses. Jay Forrester's *Urban Dynamics* (1969), a computer model of how cities grow and decline, turned his static maps into a growing city. The publisher Broderbund kept pushing for a win and lose structure; he wanted something open-ended, more of a toy, and SimCity (1989) came out through Maxis instead. Losing his home in the 1991 Oakland hills firestorm, and rebuilding, fed the "virtual doll house" that became The Sims. He has called Christopher Alexander's *A Pattern Language* probably its primary inspiration (ICON interview). He left Electronic Arts in April 2009 to run Stupid Fun Club, a think tank EA co-owned. With Lauren Elliott he co-founded Gallium Studios, whose Proxi, a game built from the player's own memories, was announced at GDC in March 2018 and has no release window.

**How he thinks.** Wikipedia's label for his work is "software toys": games with no win or lose state that can be played indefinitely. His central idea is that the real game runs in the player's head. People form a theory of the hidden rules, run an experiment, and revise; many have tried to reverse-engineer the simulator just for the fun of it. His "Simulator Effect" names the result: players imagine the simulation is far deeper and richer than it is (secondhand summaries, paraphrased). From Alexander he took a design question: what human interaction is this space for (ICON, paraphrased).

**How he innovates.** He inverts. The tool became the game (the level editor). The win state was removed and the player supplies the goals. Ideas come from outside games, in systems science, architecture and a personal loss, and he turns a dry model into something a person can hold and break.

**Core loop and the psychology it uses.** Build or change something, watch the system respond, read why, adjust. It uses curiosity, authorship and hypothesis testing. The pull is the gap between what you expected and what the model did.

**Quality bar.**
1. A person can state one rule of the system after ten minutes, and the rule is right.
2. Each action produces a visible consequence the person can trace back to it.
3. There is no single correct way to play; the person can set their own goal.
4. The model in the person's head never makes a promise the real model cannot keep.

**Lens questions.**
- What experiment can the person run here, and where do they read the result?
- What will they imagine this does that it does not do?
- Who sets the goal: us, or the person?
- What outside discipline is this model borrowed from, and is the borrowing honest?

**Example notes on Atüned.**
- Through Wright: the coherence number will get the Simulator Effect. People will believe it knows more than it does. Under rule 6, tapping it must show what it is made of, or the imagined model becomes a false claim.
- Through Wright: the streak "needs a WIN behind it" (bible 241, open). Ask first whether it needs a win at all. Goals already run by day, week and month; let the person's own goal be the win.
- Through Wright: the Descent springs back to the real coherence when released, "so you can look but you cannot pretend" (bible 675). That is his principle exactly. Apply it everywhere a person can drag or preview.

### Raph Koster

**Who and why.** The designer and theorist who wrote *A Theory of Fun for Game Design*. This is the lens for why a loop stops being fun, and what it should be teaching.

**Background.** He holds an MFA in poetry. He joined Origin Systems in 1995 as lead designer of Ultima Online, which opened online persistent worlds to a mass audience. In 2000 he moved to Verant Interactive as creative director of Star Wars Galaxies, then served as chief creative officer of Sony Online Entertainment from June 2003 to March 2006 (his CV). He founded Metaplace, a platform for user-made virtual worlds, in 2006, and Playable Worlds in 2018, where he is CEO and creative director of the sandbox online world Stars Reach. *A Theory of Fun* (Paraglyph Press, November 2004) grew out of a talk at the Austin Game Conference in 2003; O'Reilly publishes a later edition.

**How he thinks.** The brain is a pattern-recognition machine, and games are systems built to teach patterns. "Fun is just another word for learning" (the line as collected at https://www.goodreads.com/author/quotes/10980.Raph_Koster). Once the pattern is mastered, the game goes boring; boredom is the signal that the design has failed to unfold anything new. Challenge has to steer between deprivation and overload. In his ten-years-later retrospective he says fun is not the same thing as flow. Ian Bogost is among those who have criticised his focus on fun as the main purpose of games.

**How he innovates.** He treats creativity as a skill, not a gift. His GDC Next 2014 talk "Practical Creativity" lays out the habits: break a game down into its craft elements, choose ambitious and unusual themes, keep simple daily habits, and rely on "scenius" (a creative scene) and collaborators. He stopped the talk to make the audience generate ideas on the spot.

**Core loop and the psychology it uses.** Meet a pattern, try, fail, see the pattern, master it, then get a harder variant. It uses the pleasure of comprehension. The design job is to keep a next pattern always just out of reach.

**Quality bar.**
1. Name what the person learns in week one, and what new thing they learn in week four.
2. When a person stops coming back, the team can name the pattern they ran out of.
3. A new variant appears before mastery turns into routine.
4. The difficulty is neither starved nor flooded.

**Lens questions.**
- What is this teaching?
- At what point has the person learned everything here?
- What is the next pattern after that?
- Is the repetition practice, or just repetition?
- Which elements could be broken out and recombined into something new?

**Example notes on Atüned.**
- Through Koster: the release stem never changes, by design. Then the novelty has to live somewhere else. Name what day 30 teaches that day 3 did not, for example a pattern across addresses the person could not see at first. If nothing, boredom will arrive on schedule.
- Through Koster: the retention walk measures who comes back. Add a measure of what the person can now see or do that they could not on day one. Return without learning is habit, not fun.
- Through Koster: "Practical Creativity" says break it into elements. Take the six phases apart and ask which two could trade places or merge, for example REFRAME during REST.

**Uncertain.** One summary dates the book to 2005. The year of the O'Reilly edition: UNVERIFIED. Ultima Online's release year (1997) was not confirmed by a result. The "boredom is the signal" wording comes from a review's extract, so it is paraphrased here. Metaplace's peak world count varies by source (40,000 or 70,000).

**Better candidates?** Robin Hunicke. She co-wrote the MDA framework (Mechanics, Dynamics, Aesthetics, 2004, with Marc LeBlanc and Robert Zubek), and her doctoral work built Hamlet, a dynamic difficulty system on the Half-Life engine.

### Jenova Chen

**Who and why.** The designer of flOw, Flower and Journey, who wrote his thesis on flow. This is the lens for difficulty the person tunes without noticing, and for designing to a target emotion.

**Background.** His thesis "Flow in Games" came out of USC's Interactive Media program (called an MFA by some sources, a master's by others), with the game flOw as its test: a Flash version in 2006, then a PlayStation 3 version in 2007. He turned it into "Flow in games (and everything else)", *Communications of the ACM*, April 2007. He and Kellee Santiago founded thatgamecompany in 2006. Flower followed in 2009, Journey in 2012, and Sky: Children of the Light in 2019, first on iOS.

**How he thinks.** Keep each player in their own flow zone, the band where challenge matches ability; the article's abstract says a well-designed game "transports its players to their personal Flow Zones, delivering genuine feelings of pleasure and happiness" (search extract of https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145%2F1232743.1232769). He argues that hidden, formula-driven difficulty felt wrong: the system's false reactions broke his sense of control, a condition of flow (GameDev.net interview, paraphrased). So he built what he calls Active Dynamic Difficulty Adjustment: the choice is put inside the core action. In flOw you only swim and eat, and by diving between layers or choosing what to eat, you set your own difficulty, mostly without noticing. For Journey he started from an emotion and cut whatever produced the wrong one. Players pushing each other with physics was one such cut (DICE 2013, The Escapist).

**How he innovates.** He removes what every other game assumes. Journey began from loneliness he felt playing World of Warcraft, and from the observation that online players were seen as mean. He removed competition, weapons, lobbies, names and voice chat; what remained was jumping and a chirp. Meeting another player gives you energy. The DICE 2013 report is headlined "Journey is a Game About Strangers" (https://www.escapistmagazine.com/d-i-c-e-2013-jenova-chen-journey-is-a-game-about-strangers/). He also compares the arc he wanted to a three-act film structure, with pacing that stretches like a rubber band for each player (WhatCulture, paraphrased).

**Core loop and the psychology it uses.** Act, feel the response, choose how deep to go, keep moving. It uses flow (Csikszentmihalyi), control, and companionship without words.

**Quality bar.**
1. A person changes their own difficulty through the core action, not through a menu.
2. The team can name the one emotion each section is for, and it shows up in playtests.
3. Any feature that produces the wrong feeling is cut, however good it is.
4. The arc across a session has a shape: rise, valley, peak.

**Lens questions.**
- What is the target emotion here, in one word?
- Where is the difficulty dial, and is it inside the action or outside it?
- What feature is here because games usually have it?
- What does this session look like as a curve?
- When the person loses control, do they notice?

**Example notes on Atüned.**
- Through Chen: speed and pattern count are set in a menu at PICK. Move the choice into the action: in the middle of a release, let the person go one pattern deeper or surface into REST, with no menu.
- Through Chen: the summary carries a rebound warning. Test it for one feeling. If people read threat instead of care, it breaks the arc right at the peak. Keep the information and change the moment it arrives.
- Through Chen: draw the six phases as an emotional curve. REST for 120 seconds is the valley. Is SUMMARY the peak, or a report?

### Tetsuya Mizuguchi

**Who and why.** The designer of Rez, Lumines and Tetris Effect, who builds games around synesthesia, one sense triggering another. This is the lens for feedback you feel with the whole body.

**Background.** He joined Sega in 1990 and made racing games, among them Sega Rally (arcade 1994, Saturn 1995). He became the creative head of Sega's United Game Artists, which made Space Channel 5 and then Rez (2001). Rez was conceived in 1994 and 1995 and went into production in 1999. He left Sega in 2003, founded Q Entertainment, and released Lumines on PSP in 2004, followed by Child of Eden. He founded Enhance in 2014. Rez Infinite and its new level, Area X, came to PlayStation VR, with a Synesthesia Suit of 26 vibrators and LED lights shown at Roppongi Hills. Tetris Effect followed on PS4 on November 9, 2018.

**How he thinks.** Two sources keep returning. Kandinsky, who tried to make painting evoke sound, shaped Rez's wireframe look. The Street Parade in Zurich (1997 or 1998) showed him hundreds of thousands of people moved by driving beats matched to light and abstract images. He wanted a game that puts players in a trance, where shooting enemies makes the music. At GDC he said "The game should feel like a new media form" (as reported at https://www.killscreen.com/synesthesia-and-vibrators-a-history-of-innovation-from-rez/). Enhance's GDC 2019 talk on Tetris Effect credits long pre-production, prototyping, iteration and user testing.

**How he innovates.** He leaves a known mechanic almost alone and changes its senses. Rez is a simple rail shooter; Tetris Effect is Tetris. The new layer is sound, light, rhythm and touch, all answering the player's own actions. Then he adds one twist: in Tetris Effect the Zone lets you stop time and clear more lines than normal play allows. The producer Mark MacDonald says the aim was a zen-like flow state, with the game kept recognisably Tetris.

**Core loop and the psychology it uses.** Act on the beat, hear and see the world answer, act again. It uses rhythm entrainment and flow, and the player's sense of authorship: the music is partly theirs.

**Quality bar.**
1. Every action has an answer in at least two senses, on the same beat.
2. The core mechanic is still recognisable underneath the effects.
3. A person loses track of time inside the loop.
4. There is one moment where time itself changes.

**Lens questions.**
- What does this action sound like? What does it feel like in the hands?
- Is the feedback on a beat, or does it arrive when it arrives?
- What happens to the senses when the person is doing well?
- What is our Zone: the one moment time changes?
- Strip the effects: is the mechanic still the same?

**Example notes on Atüned.**
- Through Mizuguchi: the ring opens on RELEASE and closes on REFRAME. Give opening and closing a sound and a pulse on one beat, so the person feels the release rather than reads it.
- Through Mizuguchi: the awareness ring breathes on a 9.6 second cycle (bible 780). Lock the release cadence to that breath, so the loop and the body share one tempo.
- Through Mizuguchi: REST for 120 seconds could be Atüned's Zone, the moment time slows and everything quiets. Say so on screen, and let nothing else move.

## Part 2 · Video game art directors: how the look carries emotion and stays readable

### Fumito Ueda

**Who and why.** The director of Ico and Shadow of the Colossus, known for "design by subtraction." This is the lens for what to remove, and for what the remainder then owes.

**Background.** Born April 19, 1970, in Tatsuno, Hyōgo (Wikipedia). He worked for about a year and a half at the studio WARP, then at Sony, where he led Team Ico: Ico (2001), Shadow of the Colossus (2005) and The Last Guardian (2016, after more than a decade in development). Former Team Ico members formed genDESIGN in 2014. His next game, revealed at The Game Awards 2024 as "Project: Robot", is now titled gen ATLAS, published by Epic Games.

**How he thinks.** At GDC 2004, with producer Kenji Kaido, he presented the method on slides titled "Subtracting Design": actively reduce the number of elements to raise the overall quality. Their top priority was "increasing reality" and "reducing unreality." The aim was immersion, "a reality you feel inside your dream", under the heading "Emotional Reality != Photo-Realistic Reality" (all from the slide transcript at https://tale-of-tales.com/tales/ueda/transcript.txt). The slides warn the method is risky: what remains must be much denser. Ico began as "boy meets girl", with holding hands as the core action. He has said his worlds came from functional need rather than backstory, and that Ico's lack of a text tutorial was deliberate, though he later thought a kinder approach would have been better (2011, paraphrased).

**How he innovates.** The new thing is what is missing. Secondary accounts list what Ico cut: the health meter, the inventory screen, most combat, most music, and hint-giving characters. The one remaining act, holding a hand, then carries the whole game.

**How the look carries emotion and stays readable.** Large, quiet spaces, washed light, and very few objects, so the eye goes to the one figure that matters. Readability comes from emptiness: when only one thing moves, nobody has to search.

**Quality bar.**
1. Name the one emotion. Every element on screen sharpens it or is gone.
2. What remains is denser and better made than before the cut.
3. Nothing on screen breaks the reality of the world, including the interface.
4. The core action is a gesture people know from life.

**Lens questions.**
- What can be taken out, and what must get better because it was?
- Which on-screen element is "unreality": there for the system, not the world?
- What is our holding hands?
- Is this real emotionally, even if it is not literal?

**Example notes on Atüned.**
- Through Ueda: "Ship the trace, cut the map" (bible 841, open) is Subtracting Design. His condition comes with it: if the map goes, the trace must carry the detail the map held.
- Through Ueda: nine tools became four doors (bible 434). Go one step further on Home: what remains that does not serve the one feeling Atüned is for?
- Through Ueda: Atüned's holding hands may be the single tap that opens a ring. Make that one gesture the best-made thing in the product.

### Matt Nava

**Who and why.** The art director of Flower and Journey, and founder of Giant Squid (Abzû). This is the lens for colour as a script across a whole experience.

**Background.** Art director on thatgamecompany's Flower (2009) and Journey (2012). He presented "The Art of Journey" at GDC 2013, showing how its characters, creatures and architecture evolved, and published an art book of the same name. In March 2013 he and other former thatgamecompany staff founded Giant Squid in Santa Monica, where he is creative director. Abzû (2016), The Pathless (2020, an open world of about a million trees, in his words) and Sword of the Sea (2025) followed.

**How he thinks.** The point of the art is to convey a mood or a feeling, not to decorate (Digital Trends, paraphrased). His Journey tenth-anniversary retrospective shows the method in single decisions: he painted the ceremonial tower in cool colours, then switched it to warm to serve the game's larger colour script, and he saved bright blue skies for the ending as a reward, putting a green sky in an earlier scene instead. Lighting and fog are his main tools for emotional mood (GamesBeat, paraphrased).

**How he innovates.** By reacting to his last world. After three years in a desert "void of life", he wanted a vivid ocean full of it, and drew on scuba diving off the California coast and a friendly encounter with a wild sea lion. Sword of the Sea came from being a snowboarder and a surfer. The idea starts in a bodily memory, then gets its own palette.

**How the look carries emotion and stays readable.** A deliberately limited palette, a colour plan for the whole journey, strong silhouettes and readable spaces that invite you forward without explanation. Emotion comes from where colour is spent; readability from what is held back.

**Quality bar.**
1. There is a colour script for the whole experience, and each screen knows its place in it.
2. The most rewarding colour is saved for the moment that earns it.
3. The silhouette of the key element reads at a glance against any background.
4. Light and atmosphere carry the mood before any word does.

**Lens questions.**
- Where on the colour script is this screen?
- What colour are we saving, and for what?
- What does this look like as a silhouette?
- Does the space pull the eye forward without a label?
- What bodily memory is this world built from?

**Example notes on Atüned.**
- Through Nava: write a colour script for the six phases. If PICK already uses the full palette, SUMMARY and a full ring have nothing left to spend. Hold the brightest value back for done.
- Through Nava: the ring computed correctly but drew at a 1.7 pixel stroke on a 34 pixel mark, and nobody could see it (bible 512). Silhouette first: the ring must read before anything inside it.
- Through Nava: the body figure is Atüned's landscape. Light it the way he lights a scene, so the loaded band is where the light goes, not where a label points.

### Jen Zee

**Who and why.** Supergiant Games' art director: Bastion, Transistor, Pyre, Hades and Hades II. This is the lens for a strong, painterly look that still answers to the game design.

**Background.** Before Supergiant she designed isometric environments at Gaia Online. She joined Supergiant in March 2010 as its third employee, first as a contractor on Bastion, and was soon hired as art director. On Bastion and Transistor she also made all the 2D environment and interface art herself. Bastion (2011), Transistor (2014), Pyre (2017) and Hades (2020) followed, then Hades II, released in full on PC and Switch on September 25, 2025, with PlayStation and Xbox on April 14, 2026.

**How she thinks.** "We value artistic integrity and excellence in artistic craft at Supergiant, however we're first and foremost a game design-led team" (headline quote of https://mcvuk.com/business-news/behind-the-art-of-hades-we-value-artistic-integrity-and-excellence-in-artistic-craft-at-supergiant-however-were-first-and-foremost-a-game-design-lead-team/). She says she makes games to build engaging worlds, and that goal does not start with appearance. She loves colour but uses muted palettes when the story is serious.

**How she innovates.** Each game reacts against the last and borrows from art history. Transistor deliberately pushed back on Bastion's bright palette toward a darker, controlled one, drawing on Art Nouveau, Klimt, Mucha and Waterhouse (whose muted palette with vibrant atmosphere she cites). Hades was first planned as painterly; the team moved to pen and ink when the story and tone changed in pre-production, a look one essay links to Mike Mignola and which also took less time per model. For Hades II she gave the witches agile, poised bodies modelled on Muay Thai fighters rather than bulk.

**How the look carries emotion and stays readable.** Saturated colour on a controlled ground, heavy ink line and clear shapes in a fast top-down game, with large painted portraits for the slow moments of dialogue. The painterly part lives where the person has time to look; the play view stays clear.

**Quality bar.**
1. Every visual choice can be traced to a design need.
2. Each project has a palette argument: what it is for and what it is against.
3. Fast moments read by shape and value; detail goes where the person has time to look.
4. A character's body tells you how it moves before it moves.

**Lens questions.**
- What design need does this visual serve?
- What is this palette reacting against?
- Which moments are fast, and which give the person time to look?
- What art-historical source is this drawing from, and is it the right one?
- Could the style change if the tone changes, and how late?

**Example notes on Atüned.**
- Through Zee: the strip mark carries two readings at once, a ring for released and a disc for impairment (bible 309). Test whether people read both at the size shown. If they read one, the design need is not met, however good the mark looks.
- Through Zee: the palette tool lists 38 colours that need a ruling in the two themes. Give each theme a palette argument of its own, rather than letting the dark one be the light one inverted.
- Through Zee: the Avatar is Atüned's portrait moment, the place a person has time to look. Spend the painterly detail there, and keep the release loop plain and fast to read.

### Ken Wong

**Who and why.** The lead designer and artist of Monument Valley, then director of Florence. This is the lens for every frame as a picture, and for interaction that carries the feeling.

**Background.** An Australian artist, born in Adelaide according to a fan wiki. He was art director on Alice: Madness Returns (2011) at Spicy Horse in Shanghai, and is credited in its art book. At ustwo, a UX studio in London, his concept drawings shaped Monument Valley (2014). He had no formal title and later called himself lead designer. The game won an Apple Design Award and Apple's iPad Game of the Year. In 2016 he set up Mountains in Melbourne. Its first game, Florence (Annapurna Interactive, iOS February 2018), won an Apple Design Award.

**How he thinks.** His GDC Europe 2014 talk was titled "Designing Monument Valley: Less Game, More Experience." Being inside a UX studio led the team to treat the game as a user experience, to trade hard puzzles for an aesthetic journey, and to keep it short but sublime, which won new audiences. He wanted every frame to be worthy of public display (Wikipedia, paraphrased). The look drew on Escher, Japanese prints, minimalist sculpture and indie games, and levels were sketched straight in the engine rather than on paper. For Florence the stated aim was to explore emotions rather than achieve goals (paraphrased).

**How he innovates.** He makes the interaction be the feeling. Florence began from the nervousness of a first meeting and asked whether her speech could be a jigsaw that gets easier over time; early versions were all jigsaws. As the relationship changes, the actions change with it.

**How the look carries emotion and stays readable.** Flat colour fields, isometric geometry, one small figure, generous empty space. Every screen is composed as a print, and because there is so little in it, the path and the next move are obvious.

**Quality bar.**
1. Any frame, captured at random, could hang on a wall.
2. A person who never plays games finishes it.
3. The interaction itself enacts the emotion of the moment.
4. It is short, and nothing in it is filler.

**Lens questions.**
- Would this frame work as a print?
- Who is this for, and could someone who has never played a game finish it?
- What does the gesture feel like, and does that match what the moment means?
- What would we cut to make it shorter and better?
- Is the person winning here, or feeling something?

**Example notes on Atüned.**
- Through Wong: capture one frame from each of the six phases. If REST or SUMMARY would not hang on a wall, it is not finished.
- Through Wong: Florence's talk got easier as the couple grew close. Let the release interaction change as an address loosens, using the real percent released, so the gesture itself reports progress.
- Through Wong: the rings came last on comprehension in the measured arcs (bible 838). Less game, more experience: show the person a moment, not the ontology.

### Conrad Roset

**Who and why.** The Barcelona watercolourist who directed Gris and Neva at Nomada Studio. This is the lens for colour returning as the measure of healing.

**Background.** A watercolour and ink artist based in Barcelona. He started his career working with Zara for about a year, then went freelance. He met Adrián Cuevas and Roger Mendoza, two Ubisoft developers with AAA experience, and with them formed Nomada Studio. Gris, directed and painted by Roset, came out on December 13, 2018 through Devolver Digital and has sold over a million copies. Neva (2024, also Devolver), about a young woman and her wolf across four seasons, added combat.

**How he thinks.** The first idea for Gris was to move from black and white to as many colours as possible, and the story was built around that progression (Gamelab 2019 interview, paraphrased). He credits the story to the aesthetics and the mechanics together, with music playing a key part. The girl cannot die, and there are no conventional enemies to fight.

**How he innovates.** He brings an illustrator's medium into games whole, watercolour pooling as on paper, and makes the medium's missing colour the progress system. The world is grey; colour comes back as she moves forward, and her new abilities come from her dress. One critic reads each colour as a stage of grief.

**How the look carries emotion and stays readable.** Large washes, a tiny figure, and at most one new colour introduced at a time. Because colour is scarce, each new one is unmistakable, and the player reads progress without a number.

**Quality bar.**
1. Progress can be read from the picture alone, without a counter.
2. Each new colour arrives on its own, with a reason.
3. The medium (watercolour, ink) is visible, not just imitated.
4. Nobody fails; difficulty never punishes the feeling the piece is about.

**Lens questions.**
- If we took every number away, could you still see progress?
- What colour is earned here, and by what?
- Does the hand of the artist show?
- Is there any way to fail this, and should there be?

**Example notes on Atüned.**
- Through Roset: let colour return to the body figure as each address is released, driven by the real percent released (bible 270). Colour then becomes the measure itself, which keeps rule 6 instead of decorating around it.
- Through Roset: no one can fail a release session. Check that the rebound warning in the summary does not read as a failure state.
- Through Roset: the first session could open almost grey, so the first released address is the first real colour the person sees.
