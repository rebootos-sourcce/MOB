# Emmy's lenses for UI and product design (five people) and creative studios (five agencies), used to review Atüned and The Mechanics of Being
Researched 2026-10-15.
Lenses, not authorities: Emmy reviews through them, she never speaks for them.

**How this was researched.** Opening pages directly failed from this machine (every site returned a DNS error), so each fact below was confirmed through web search results that quote or summarise the pages listed under each entry. No page was read in full. There are only a few direct quotes, and each gives the URL its words came from. Everything else is paraphrase. UNVERIFIED marks a claim that no result confirmed.

**How to use a lens.** A review note names the lens and the test that failed, then the fix: "Through Tufte: the number has no comparison. Put yesterday beside it." It never says what the person would think or feel.

## Part 1 · People: UI, UX and product design

### Don Norman

**Who and why.** He is the cognitive scientist who gave product design its working vocabulary. This is the lens for whether a person can tell what to do, and can tell that it worked.

**Background.** Born 1935. Electrical engineering at MIT, then a PhD in psychology at Penn. Founding director of the Design Lab at UC San Diego, now professor emeritus. Vice president at Apple (dates UNVERIFIED), later an executive at HP, and co-founder of Nielsen Norman Group. *The Psychology of Everyday Things* (1988) was renamed *The Design of Everyday Things*. The revised edition of 2013 added the idea of signifiers and two new chapters. *Emotional Design* came out in 2004, and *Design for a Better World* (MIT Press, 2023) argues for design centred on humanity as a whole.

**How he thinks.** Design is a conversation between a thing and a mind. **Affordance**: what an object lets a particular person do (a relationship, not a property). **Signifier**: the visible cue that shows where and how to act. **Mapping**: the layout of the controls matches the layout of their effects. **Constraints**: cut down the possible actions so the right one is obvious. **Feedback**: full, immediate information about the new state. **Conceptual model**: the person's working story of how the thing works, which the design has to teach. *Emotional Design* adds three levels: visceral (the reaction to look and feel, before thought), behavioural (how it works in use) and reflective (what it means to the person afterwards). When someone makes an error, he blames the design before the person.

**Quality bar.**
1. With no instructions, a first-time user can say what each screen is for and what to press next.
2. Every action gets feedback that names the new state, in the word the control used.
3. A control is laid out like what it changes: left-side controls act on the left side of the body.
4. The person can say in one sentence how the number is made, and the sentence is right.

**Lens questions.**
- What is the signifier here, and would someone who has never heard the word "fetter" see it?
- What model of the product does this screen teach, and does it match what the code computes?
- What changed after the tap, and how does the person know?
- Where would a careful person still go wrong, and whose fault is that?

**Example notes on Atüned.**
- Through Norman: RELEASE and RITUAL stay hidden until the stage before them has produced something. A sequence is a good constraint, but a hidden tab has no signifier. Show all four steps, mark the locked ones, and give each one line saying what unlocks it.
- Through Norman: SQ appears on Home and in the summary with two different definitions (pass 6, G-1.1). Two meanings under one label break the person's model of the product at its root. Use one label with one formula, or give the two numbers two labels.
- Through Norman: Release confirms as "Released", using the same word. Good feedback: make every drawer tool confirm that way.

### Susan Kare

**Who and why.** She is the artist who drew the original Macintosh interface. This is the lens for whether a symbol carries its meaning at a small size with very little.

**Background.** Born 1954 in Ithaca, New York. Her friend Andy Hertzfeld brought her to Apple's Macintosh group in 1982 and suggested she work on graph paper, one square per pixel. The Computer History Museum calls her the sole creator of the Mac's screen graphics at that point. Her work there includes the Happy Mac, the bomb, the command key symbol (adapted from a Swedish sign marking places of interest at campsites), Clarus the Dogcow, and the bitmap fonts Chicago, Geneva and New York. She then worked at NeXT and founded Susan Kare Design in 1988. She drew hundreds of Facebook virtual gifts between 2006 and 2010, was a creative director at Pinterest, and was a design architect at Niantic. She received the AIGA Medal in 2018.

**How she thinks.** An icon should work like a road sign, not an illustration: one idea, stated clearly enough to remember, with nothing extra (her framing from several interviews, paraphrased here). The grid (16 by 16 or 32 by 32 pixels, black and white, no smoothing) is a discipline because it forces the question of what matters. She takes metaphors from everyday life: signage, packaging, warning labels. Nouns are easy and verbs are hard: "Undo is especially hard" (Adweek). She still aims for clarity and simplicity now that colour and resolution no longer force them.

**Quality bar.**
1. At 16 pixels, it still reads as that thing and not as something else.
2. With the colour removed, it still reads.
3. Shown without a label to someone outside the product, they can name the action.
4. Every icon in the set looks drawn by one hand on one grid.

**Lens questions.**
- Is this icon a noun or a verb? If it is a verb, what does the person see happening?
- Which everyday sign does it borrow, and will Atüned's buyer recognise it?
- Does the warmth come from the drawing itself, or from the decoration around it?

**Example notes on Atüned.**
- Through Kare: the 17 drawer tools (compass, psyche, patternmap, polarity and the rest) are verbs dressed up as nouns. Draw each one as what happens, at 24 px on one grid, then test them without labels. If nobody can name the action, the tool's name is the problem, not the icon.
- Through Kare: the body figure is the product's main symbol. Draw it at 32 by 32 with one band lit. If people can still say which band is loaded at that size, the figure can serve as the app icon and as the base of the icon set.
- Through Kare: Release is the product's most important verb. Give it a drawn sign of its own, not a generic button.

### Bill Atkinson

**Who and why.** He was the engineer and designer behind the Mac's graphics, and he later let people who were not programmers build software. This is the lens for tools that hand power to the person using them.

**Background.** 1951 to 2025. He died on June 5, 2025, of pancreatic cancer, aged 74. He was principal designer of the Apple Lisa's graphical interface. He wrote QuickDraw, the graphics engine under the Mac, including its "regions". He wrote MacPaint (1984), which brought the tool palette, the lasso and pixel editing to a mass audience. He then made HyperCard (1987): stacks of linked cards scripted in HyperTalk, a scripting language close to plain English. In 1990 he left Apple and co-founded General Magic with Andy Hertzfeld and Marc Porat. Later a nature photographer: *Within the Stone* (2004).

**How he thinks.** Two habits are on record. **Make the hard part fast and simple underneath so the surface can be generous.** Hertzfeld's Folklore stories record that in 1981 Steve Jobs pushed him to add rounded rectangles to QuickDraw, and he got them drawing almost as fast as plain ones. In 1982 he rewrote the region engine to be simpler and about six times faster, then entered his week's output on Apple's lines-of-code form as minus 2,000. **Let the people who know the subject build.** HyperCard let teachers, artists and small businesses make their own software. He later said he missed the mark by keeping links on one machine: Apple's box-centred culture stopped him from seeing the network (from secondary accounts of a 2002 Wired interview).

**Quality bar.**
1. Someone with no training makes something of their own in the first session.
2. Every available action is visible in a palette. Nothing is buried.
3. Removing a feature can make the product simpler than adding one would.

**Lens questions.**
- What could a practitioner build here that we did not plan for?
- Is this more code doing less, or less code doing more?
- Which assumption about the box (one session, one device, one client) will the person's real world break?

**Example notes on Atüned.**
- Through Atkinson: the practitioner spec lists what a practitioner can view. It should also list what they can make: a saved release sequence, a card set of their own, a protocol for one client. The person who knows the subject is the author.
- Through Atkinson: there are 17 tools, and 8 of them are reached only through data attributes (APP_MAP). Before adding an 18th, look for the minus 2,000: which two tools are really the same tool?
- Through Atkinson: the drawer is a list, while MacPaint's palette was a map. Show every tool at once, grouped by what it acts on (the story, the body, the number).

### Edward Tufte

**Who and why.** He is the statistician who set the standard for showing data honestly and densely. This is the lens for the coherence number and every chart around it.

**Background.** He studied statistics at Stanford and took a PhD in political science at Yale. He taught at Princeton and then Yale, where he is professor emeritus of political science, statistics and computer science. In 1983 he founded Graphics Press to publish *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* himself (1983, second edition 2001). Then came *Envisioning Information* (1990), *Visual Explanations* (1997), *Beautiful Evidence* (2006) and *Seeing with Fresh Eyes* (2020). His analysis of the Boeing and NASA slides made while the Columbia shuttle was damaged appeared in the accident board's final report, and it was followed by his 32-page essay *The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint*.

**How he thinks.** **Data-ink**: he defines it as the "proportion of a graphic's ink devoted to the non-redundant display of data-information" (quoted at https://clauswilke.com/dataviz/balance-data-context). Raise that share, within reason, by erasing decoration, heavy grids and boxes. **Chartjunk**: decoration that carries no information. **Small multiples**: the same small chart repeated across one variable, so the eye can compare. **Sparklines**: small, word-sized graphics set inside a sentence. **The aim**: the most ideas, in the shortest time, with the least ink, in the smallest space. **Formats shape thinking**: a hierarchy of bullet points can hide the one fact that matters.

**Quality bar.**
1. Every mark either encodes data or is needed to read it. Strip the rest and nothing is lost.
2. A number always appears with its comparison: compared with what, since when, and how much change is only noise.
3. States sit side by side at the same scale, not one at a time behind taps.
4. Words, numbers and images sit together, with no legend to look up.

**Lens questions.**
- Compared with what: yesterday, the person's baseline, or nothing at all?
- Which glow on this screen carries no data?
- Could this be small multiples, such as seven days of the body map in one row?
- What precision is real here, and does the display claim more than that?

**Example notes on Atüned.**
- Through Tufte: the coherence number is the ceiling divided by resistance (COHERENCE_LOGIC.md). Put the terms beside it as a line of small figures, so the person can see which one moved it. A big number standing alone asks for trust it has not earned.
- Through Tufte: seven small body maps at one scale would show a week's pattern that a single map hides. A sparkline beside each band would add its history.
- Through Tufte and rule 6: the rim-light study reports that 100% would "keep opening for it". If that 1,000-seat panel was simulated, say so right next to the figure. A modelled number dressed as a measured one is exactly the failure he documents.

**Sources.** https://www.edwardtufte.com/book/the-visual-display-of-quantitative-information/ · https://www.edwardtufte.com/notebook/chartjunk/ · https://www.edwardtufte.com/notebook/sparklines-history-by-tufte-1324-to-now/ · https://www.edwardtufte.com/books/ · https://clauswilke.com/dataviz/balance-data-context · https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/impactevaluations/timeless-principles-for-better-figure-design · https://archive.skeptic.com/archive/lectures/beautiful-evidence · https://creativehalloffame.org/inductees/edward-tufte/

### Bret Victor

**Who and why.** He is the interface researcher whose principle is that people who make things should see the effect of each change immediately. This is the lens for whether Atüned lets a person feel cause and effect.

**Background.** At Apple from 2007 to 2010, according to his CV, he was a founding member of "HID Proto", a team that prototyped interface concepts for experimental hardware, and he says his early concepts shaped the iPad. From 2011 to 2013 he did independent research, including interactive design on the climate book *Our Choice*. From 2014 he led the research group that became Dynamicland (earlier called CDG and HARC), co-founded with Alan Kay: a non-profit lab building a "humane dynamic medium". His key works are on worrydream.com: *Magic Ink* (2005), *Explorable Explanations* and *Kill Math* (2011), *Up and Down the Ladder of Abstraction* (October 2011), the talk *Inventing on Principle* (CUSEC, January 20, 2012) and *Learnable Programming* (2012).

**How he thinks.** Find a principle you believe in and invent from it. His principle is that makers need an immediate connection to what they make: change something and see the effect at once. Most software is *information software*, used to learn, compare and decide, so it should infer context from history and surroundings instead of asking. Understanding comes from moving **up and down the ladder of abstraction**: control one concrete case, generalise from it, then step back down. Text should be a place to think in, not just something to read.

**Quality bar.**
1. When an input changes, the output changes at the same moment and in view.
2. The person can drag a value and watch the result, rather than typing and waiting.
3. State is visible. Nothing important happens off-screen.
4. The app never asks for anything it could have worked out from what the person already did.

**Lens questions.**
- When the person edits one word of their story, where do they see the body and the number respond, and how fast?
- What state is hidden here, and how would we show it?
- What are we asking for that we already know?
- Is this screen at the level of one moment or the whole pattern, and can the person move between the two?

**Example notes on Atüned.**
- Through Victor: STORY and IMPRINTS are separate tabs. Put the story and the body figure in one view, so each sentence lights its band as it is written.
- Through Victor: on the coherence screen, let the person drag SQ from 0 to 10 and watch the number respond, so the formula becomes something they feel. Label it clearly as a what-if and never as their reading (rule 6).
- Through Victor (*Magic Ink*): the app already knows which band is loaded. Offer the one tool that answers that band, instead of a list of 17.

**Sources.** https://worrydream.com/ · https://worrydream.com/cv/ · https://worrydream.com/InventingOnPrinciple/ · https://worrydream.com/MagicInk/ · https://worrydream.com/LearnableProgramming/ · https://worrydream.com/LadderOfAbstraction/ · https://worrydream.com/ExplorableExplanations/ · https://worrydream.com/KillMath/ · https://dynamicland.org/about-us/ · https://dynamicland.org/2019/TTIV_talk.pdf · https://hacks.mozilla.org/2012/04/bret-victors-inventing-on-principle-and-a-few-things-it-inspired/

**Uncertain.** His Apple title ("Human Interface Inventor" is often quoted): UNVERIFIED. Dynamicland's location in Oakland and its funders: UNVERIFIED. Some sources date *Magic Ink* to 2006, but his own site says 2005.

## Part 2 · Studios and creative agencies

### Pentagram

**Founding and people.** Founded in London on June 12, 1972, by Theo Crosby (architect), Alan Fletcher, Colin Forbes and Mervyn Kurlansky (graphic designers) and Kenneth Grange (industrial designer). It grew out of Fletcher/Forbes/Gill (1962). It now has 23 to 25 partners across several offices. Paula Scher became a partner in 1991.

**Signature work.** Scher's identity for the Public Theater, from 1994: street typography built from mismatched American wood types (drawing on Rob Roy Kelly's *American Wood Types* and Victorian theatre posters), cut-out photographs and two or three flat colours. Her 1995 posters for *Bring in 'da Noise, Bring in 'da Funk* carried the system. Later changes reportedly dropped "theater" to stress "public" (2005) and moved to the Knockout typeface (2008). Her Citi logo (1998) began as a sketch on a napkin during a meeting.

**Process and philosophy.** The firm's structure is its philosophy. Every partner is a working designer with equal equity, income and vote, and there is no CEO. Each partner runs a team and answers for its profit, while the studio space, finance and new-business staff are shared. A new partner needs a unanimous vote after a process that can take two to three years. Scher has said an all-type identity was easier to keep alive than a drawn one, because casts and scripts kept changing. Her TED talk (2008) argued that design should be serious, not solemn.

**What the best work shares.** One strong typographic or visual idea, owned by one designer, flexible enough to live through years of changing content.

**Quality bar.** (1) It still works when the content changes every week. (2) The type alone carries the voice: hide the logo and it is still recognisable. (3) The owner can state the idea in one sentence.

**Lens questions.**
- With the logo hidden, does a screen still look like Atüned?
- What is our one typographic idea, and who owns it?
- Will it survive new tools, new chapters and copy nobody has written yet?

**Example notes on Atüned.**
- Through Pentagram: the ü is the most ownable letter in the name. Build the type system from it (two dots as the paired left and right reading) and carry it across the app, the book and the store page, instead of adding a separate symbol.
- Through Pentagram: the copy is mechanical (VOICE.md, rule 2), so the type should be too. Set readings in one condensed grotesque, big and flat like the Public's street type, not in a soft wellness face.

### Wieden+Kennedy

**Founding and people.** Founded in Portland, Oregon, on April 1, 1982, by Dan Wieden (died 2022, aged 77) and David Kennedy (died 2021, aged 82). Nike was the first client. W+K is one of the largest independently owned agencies.

**Signature work.** Nike's "Just Do It" (1988), a line Wieden wrote. Old Spice's "The Man Your Man Could Smell Like" (launched online February 4, 2010, starring Isaiah Mustafa, directed by Tom Kuntz) won the Film Grand Prix at Cannes Lions in 2010. W+K Portland won three Grand Prix that week.

**Process and philosophy.** Independence is the creative condition. Wieden wanted a place where people could do their best work without the bureaucracy of big agencies: an independent creative company, not a vendor. Two house mottos: **"Walk in stupid every morning"**, on a blender-headed mannequin in the London office, meaning drop what you think you know; and **"Fail Harder"**, failure as the price of a good idea. He pushed his staff to find their own voice.

**What the best work shares.** A brand talking in a human voice, with conviction and wit, saying one thing to people who did not expect to be spoken to that way.

**Quality bar.** (1) The line could only belong to this brand. (2) A stranger feels something within ten seconds. (3) It takes a risk that could fail in public.

**Lens questions.**
- What is our three-word line, the one a person says to themselves?
- Walking in stupid: what does someone who has never heard of a fetter get from the first screen?
- Where are we playing safe because failing would be embarrassing?

**Example notes on Atüned.**
- Through W+K: "Release", then "Released", is already a voice: short, mechanical and declarative. Write the store line in that voice, as a verb and a result, not a promise of calm.
- Through W+K: the first launch shows STORY, and the drawer shows "psyche" and "patternmap". Test it on someone who has read none of the book. Every word they ask about is a word the brand has not yet earned.

### Chermayeff & Geismar & Haviv

**Founding and people.** Founded in New York in 1957 by Ivan Chermayeff and Tom Geismar, both Yale graduates, with Robert Brownjohn, who left within about three years. Sagi Haviv became a partner (2006, by one account), and his name was added to the firm's in 2013. Chermayeff died on December 2, 2017.

**Signature work.** The Chase Manhattan octagon (about 1960), Mobil, NYU, the 1986 redraw of the NBC peacock (fewer feathers, six for the network's divisions), PBS, National Geographic, the Smithsonian, and Haviv's US Open symbol.

**Process and philosophy.** There is no house style. The marks look different on purpose, because each must be a distinctive, memorable and appropriate expression of its own organisation (Geismar). Haviv uses three tests. **Appropriate**: the right personality and attitude for the business. **Distinctive**: memorable enough that people can describe it or doodle it from memory, which he calls the doodle test. **Simple**: it works at any size and on any platform, including in pixels. The order of work is research, hand sketches, the mark, then the lettering and how it is applied.

**What the best work shares.** A single idea, simple enough to draw from memory and right enough for the client that it lasts for decades without a redesign.

**Quality bar.** (1) It passes the doodle test. (2) It holds up as a favicon and on the side of a building. (3) Its personality matches the client, not a trend.

**Lens questions.**
- Can someone draw our mark after seeing it once?
- Does it work in one colour at 16 px?
- Does it read as mechanical precision, which is the brand's claim, or as calm wellness, which is the category's cliché?

**Example notes on Atüned.**
- Through CG&H: give the current app icon the doodle test with five people. If they draw a generic figure or a lotus, the mark has the category's personality, not Atüned's.
- Through CG&H: one mark has to work in the macOS dock, the menu bar, the book spine and the store. Test it at 16 px in one colour before any gradient or glow is added.

### Elastic

**Founding and people.** A design studio in Santa Monica, founded by Angus Wall (2008, according to one source). Its sister companies are the editing house Rock Paper Scissors and the effects studio a52, all owned by Wall and Linda Carlson. Jennifer Sofio Hall is executive producer. Wall is also a film editor and has won two Oscars (*The Social Network*, *The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo*).

**Signature work.** Main titles for *Carnivàle* (an Emmy for Wall) and *Game of Thrones* (Emmy, 2011): a mechanical map of the world that builds itself city by city. In 2019 that map was rebuilt to start north of the Wall and follow the threat south. *True Detective* won the Emmy in 2014. *The Crown*, *Westworld* and *American Gods* were all nominated in 2017, with Patrick Clair as creative director on several. Later work includes *Carnival Row*, *The Morning Show* and *Watchmen*.

**Process and philosophy.** A title sequence is the show's argument in miniature. *Thrones* began from George R. R. Martin's own maps, and by the final season Wall described it as no longer just a geography lesson: the map showed where the story stood. Ideas often come from the show's creators (the Muybridge reference for *Carnival Row* came from its screenwriter), and the team works through false starts until the image holds the show's dynamics.

**What the best work shares.** One visual system that explains the world and sets its tone before anyone speaks, and that can change as the story does.

**Quality bar.** (1) Afterwards, the viewer knows where they are and what kind of story this is. (2) The system updates when the world changes. (3) Nothing is decoration: every element belongs to the world.

**Lens questions.**
- What is Atüned's main title, the thing a person sees every time they open the app?
- Does it teach the layout (the body and its bands) that every later screen refers to?
- Can it change to show where this person is today?

**Example notes on Atüned.**
- Through Elastic: treat launch as a main title. A few seconds of the figure building from root to crown, ending on today's real state, teaches the map that every tool points into.
- Through Elastic: the 2019 *Thrones* map opened where the threat was. Open on the person's most loaded band, not on the same frame every time, and use only the real reading (rule 6).

### Territory Studio

**Founding and people.** Founded in London in 2010 by David Sheldon-Hicks (executive creative director, who had worked on *Casino Royale* and *The Dark Knight*), Lee Fasciani and Nick Glover. An older report also mentions small offices in New York and San Francisco.

**Signature work.** Screen graphics and fictional interfaces for *Prometheus* (2012), *Guardians of the Galaxy*, *Ex Machina*, *The Martian*, *Blade Runner 2049*, *Ad Astra*, *Mr Robot* and *The Batman*. Game interface work on *Forza Motorsport 6*, and installations including one for *Jurassic World Dominion*.

**Process and philosophy.** Research comes first, then the story. *Prometheus* was the project that set the studio's direction: the brief asked for interfaces inspired by sea creatures, and the concepts were grounded in current military and scientific thinking. *Ex Machina* needed believable near-future interfaces, so they started from how operating systems and programming work today. On *The Martian* they worked with NASA's Dave Lavery and studied how mission control decoded images from the Pathfinder probe, then pushed NASA's conventions 20 to 30 years forward. Five artists made about 400 screens over seven months, most of them interactive.

**What the best work shares.** Technology that an expert would believe and an audience can read, with its look drawn from the world of the story.

**Quality bar.** (1) A specialist in the field would accept it. (2) A viewer gets its point in one glance. (3) Its visual language comes from that world, not from generic science fiction.

**Lens questions.**
- Would a body practitioner or a physiologist find this screen credible?
- What is the research reference behind this graphic?
- Which parts are story and which are data, and does the screen say which is which?

**Example notes on Atüned.**
- Through Territory: research the body map the way they researched mission control. NERVE_PLEXUS_REFERENCE.md is the real source. Draw the bands and seats from it, so a practitioner sees anatomy and not decoration.
- Through Territory, with a caution: film interfaces only have to look true, and Atüned's have to be true (rule 6). Borrow their research discipline, never their animated readouts that are not tied to a measurement.

### Better candidates? (studios)

- **Perception** (New York, founded 2001 by Jeremy Lasky and Danny Gonzalez). They designed interfaces for Marvel from *Iron Man 2* through *Black Panther*, then the screens in the shipping GMC Hummer EV. It is the same craft as Territory's, with proof that it carries into a real product.
