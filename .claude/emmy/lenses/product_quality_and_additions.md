## How this was researched (read first)

- Web search only. Direct page fetches were blocked from this environment, so primary pages (Vitsoe, Wikipedia, Stanford, Toyota) were read through search excerpts and cross-checked against at least one other source. Where only one secondary source carried a fact, it says so.
- Quotes appear in quotation marks only where a URL carrying those exact words was found. That URL is listed. Everything else is paraphrase and is marked "paraphrase".
- "UNVERIFIED" means: widely repeated, not traced to a source in this pass. Do not quote it to Lance as fact.
- A lens describes how a person documentably thinks and works. It is a set of questions Emmy can ask. It is never "Rams would say" or "Jobs would approve".

# F. Product quality

## 1. Jony Ive

**Who and why.** Industrial designer who led Apple design from the late 1990s to 2019. Named by Lance as the bar for care and finish.

**Background.** Born 1967 in Chingford, London. Studied industrial design at Newcastle Polytechnic (now Northumbria University). Joined Apple in September 1992 and led industrial design after Steve Jobs returned in 1997. Led the design of the iMac (1998), iPod, iPhone, iPad and Apple Watch. Chief design officer from 2015 until July 2019, then founded the design collective LoveFrom with Marc Newson. In 2025 OpenAI announced it was acquiring io, the hardware startup Ive co-founded, with LoveFrom taking on design work for OpenAI. Chancellor of the Royal College of Art since 2017.

**How he thinks.**
- Materials and process before shape. He has described the team talking about processes and materials rather than shapes (paraphrase, Vanity Fair via AppleInsider). The unibody MacBook, shown in the film *Objectified* (2009), collapsed many parts into one machined part; he framed that work as figuring out a process, not only an object (paraphrase).
- Physical models change the thinking. He has said the team's thinking shifts once they hold mockups in metal or plastic (paraphrase, TechCrunch).
- Simplicity is not subtraction. From the iOS 7 introduction film, WWDC 2013: "True simplicity is derived from so much more than just the absence of clutter and ornamentation."
- Care is felt even where it is not seen. At Stripe Sessions 2025 (attendee notes, paraphrase) he used the packaging of a charging cable as an example: people can tell when care went in, even in parts they never consciously look at. He named "solving a functional imperative and we're done" as an anti-pattern, and warned that uncluttered products can be cold and soulless.
- Team ritual as part of the work: the design team took turns making Friday breakfast for everyone (Stripe 2025, secondary).

**Quality bar.**
1. The unseen parts are finished to the same standard as the seen ones.
2. Something is removed only if the thing gets better, not just emptier.
3. It has been tried as a real object at real size, not only judged as a render or a mock.
4. The way it is made is part of the design, not handed off after it.

**Lens questions.**
- Where would a person feel that nobody cared?
- Is this simple because it is understood, or because things were deleted?
- Has it been tried at real size, real speed, on the real screen?
- What is this made of (type, light, motion, sound), and is that material worked all the way through?
- Where did we stop because the function technically worked?

**Applied to Atüned.**
- When the coherence number changes, watch the change itself. If it snaps from one value to the next with no transition and no reason given, that is the unpainted back of the cabinet.
- Empty, loading and error states on the body map get the same finish as the hero screen. Check them at desktop size in both themes.
- Before calling a screen "clean", list what was removed. If a unit, a label or the source of the number went, it was carrying meaning.

## 2. Steve Jobs

**Who and why.** Apple co-founder and its CEO through the turnaround. Named by Lance as the bar for focus and product taste.

**Background.** Developed the Apple I with Steve Wozniak in 1976. Led the Macintosh team; at the team's January 1983 retreat one of his slogans was "Real Artists Ship". Pushed out of Apple in 1985 (he tells this as the second story of his 2005 Stanford commencement speech); returned in 1997 and led the comeback. Died October 5, 2011, in Palo Alto.

**How he thinks.**
- Design is function, not surface. New York Times Magazine, 2003: "Design is not just what it looks like and feels like. Design is how it works."
- Focus means saying no. At WWDC 1997, answering a developer about a cancelled technology, he said focus is about saying no (paraphrase: transcriptions of the exact words differ). Secondary accounts describe him cutting the Mac range to a two-by-two grid: consumer and pro, desktop and portable.
- Simplicity is hard thinking. BusinessWeek, May 1998: "Simple can be harder than complex. You have to work hard to get your thinking clean to make it simple."
- Lead with a product, not a survey. Same interview: "A lot of times, people don't know what they want until you show it to them." The context was focus groups, not ignoring people.
- The unseen work is done right. Biographer Walter Isaacson recounted Paul Jobs telling his son: "You've got to make the back of the fence, that nobody will see, just as good looking as the front of the fence." These are Isaacson's words for the father, not Jobs's own.
- Shipping is part of the art: "Real Artists Ship" (1983 retreat, as recorded by Andy Hertzfeld).
- Trust that the dots connect looking back (paraphrase, Stanford 2005).

**Quality bar.**
1. What it does for a person fits in one plain sentence.
2. Good things were cut so the rest could be great.
3. The hidden work is done right.
4. It ships. A great thing that never ships fails this bar.

**Lens questions.**
- What are we saying no to, by name?
- Would someone know what to do in the first ten seconds without being told?
- Where are we designing by committee or by poll instead of by conviction followed by testing?
- Is the back of the fence painted?
- When does this ship, and what stands between here and there?

**Applied to Atüned.**
- Count what a first-time user can do from the first screen. If it is more than a handful, name which ones wait for later.
- "How it works" includes understanding: on first view the coherence number says in plain words what it measures and where it comes from.
- A screen that is beautiful but not wired, or not passing the gate, has not shipped. It does not count.

## 3. Dieter Rams

**Who and why.** German industrial designer, head of design at Braun from 1961 to 1995. Added by Emmy because his ten principles are the most cited written quality bar in product design.

**Background.** At Braun he added the clear perspex lid to the SK 4 radio-phonograph (1956); the Design Museum credits the SK 4 with moving household equipment away from looking like furniture. In 1959 he began working with Vitsoe (then Vitsoe-Zapf), which led to the 606 Universal Shelving System, still in production. In the late 1970s, troubled by what he called "an impenetrable confusion of forms, colours and noises", he asked himself whether his own design was good design. His answer became the ten principles. Gary Hustwit's documentary *Rams* came out in 2018.

**How he thinks.**
- Starts from a question about the world, not a style: is this adding to the confusion or reducing it?
- The ten principles, as Vitsoe headlines them: good design is innovative; makes a product useful; is aesthetic; makes a product understandable; is unobtrusive; is honest; is long-lasting; is thorough down to the last detail; is environmentally friendly; is as little design as possible.
- "Less, but better." In Hustwit's film he extends it beyond objects to behaviour, a way to live (paraphrase).
- Innovation follows real technology and is never an end in itself (paraphrase of Vitsoe's note on principle 1).
- Self-critical. The Rams Foundation says the principles are not meant to be incontrovertible, because ideas of good design keep evolving (paraphrase). Hustwit reports Rams regrets his part in a culture of overconsumption and would not choose to be a designer again (paraphrase).
- Systems over objects: the 606 is a system people add to over decades.

**Quality bar.**
1. Honest: it does not claim to be more powerful or valuable than it is.
2. Understandable: it explains itself without a manual.
3. Unobtrusive: a tool, not a decoration.
4. Thorough down to the last detail.
5. Long-lasting: it will not look dated when the fashion moves.

**Lens questions.**
- What here is design for its own sake?
- Does the product explain itself?
- Does anything claim more than the product can deliver?
- Will this look right in ten years?
- What would "less, but better" take out?

**Applied to Atüned.**
- Principle 6 is Lance's rule 6 in design language. A coherence number shown with more precision than its inputs support is dishonest design, not only a data bug.
- Unobtrusive: glow, particles or ambient motion on the body map that do not encode any state are candidates for removal.
- Long-lasting: any colour or type choice made because it is current now is a future liability.

## 4. Taiichi Ohno

**Who and why.** Toyota engineer and executive who built the Toyota Production System. Added by Emmy because "nothing ships red" is his stop-the-line principle in software form.

**Background.** Born 1912 in Dalian, then Japanese-controlled Manchuria. Graduated from Nagoya Technical High School in 1932, joined a Toyoda textile company (sources differ on which), and moved to Toyota Motor in 1943. Rose through the machine shops to executive vice president (1975, per one source), retired in 1978 and consulted until 1982. Died May 28, 1990. His book *Toyota Production System: Beyond Large-Scale Production* appeared in Japanese in 1978 and in English (Productivity Press) in 1988.

**How he thinks.**
- Two pillars. Just-in-time: make only what is needed, when it is needed, in the amount needed. Jidoka: stop at the first abnormality so a defect is never passed on. Toyota traces jidoka to Sakichi Toyoda's loom, which stopped itself when a thread broke, and just-in-time to Kiichiro Toyoda in the 1930s. Ohno turned both into a working system.
- Anyone can stop the line. Andon cords let any operator halt production and signal a problem (secondary).
- Go and see. The "Ohno circle": he would chalk a circle on the shop floor, have a manager stand in it and watch, and send back anyone who reported seeing no problems (lean.org and others, paraphrase).
- Ask why five times, to get past the symptom to the cause. Widely credited to Ohno and taught from his book (paraphrase).
- Seven wastes: overproduction, waiting, transport, over-processing, inventory, motion, and defects (correction).

**Quality bar.**
1. A defect stops work where it is found. It is never passed downstream to be found later.
2. Every abnormality is visible the moment it happens.
3. The fix goes to the process that made the defect, not only to the defective part.
4. Nothing is built ahead of need. Unfinished work in a pile is waste.
5. Whoever judges the work has watched the real work, not a report of it.

**Lens questions.**
- Where can a defect move from one stage to the next without stopping anything?
- Who is allowed to stop the line, and do they actually do it?
- What is built and waiting? That is inventory.
- Has anyone stood in the circle and watched a real person use this, start to finish?
- Why? And why again, five times.

**Applied to Atüned.**
- The gate is jidoka. The test is not that it exists but that a red result stops a merge every time, with no override path.
- The `unwired/` folder holds work that is built but not in the build. Through this lens each item is inventory: it ships, or it gets a ruling.
- The record already shows the method working: three landing functions with zero callers passed every assertion. Why? The assertions checked that functions existed. Why? Nothing checked for a caller. The fix went to the process (a caller check in the gate), not just the three functions.

## 5. W. Edwards Deming

**Who and why.** American statistician whose teaching on variation and systems shaped Japanese and then American management. Added by Emmy because a coherence number is a measurement, and Deming is the bar for what a measurement can and cannot mean.

**Background.** Invited by the Union of Japanese Scientists and Engineers (JUSE), he arrived in June 1950 to teach statistical methods; the transcribed lectures sold in the thousands. JUSE created the Deming Prize in 1951. The NBC documentary "If Japan Can, Why Can't We?" (June 24, 1980) made him known in the US. His 14 points were first published in 1982 (MIT Center for Advanced Engineering Study) and revised as *Out of the Crisis* (1986 edition). He died in 1993.

**How he thinks.**
- The system, not the person. In the Red Bead Experiment, willing workers produce defects because the system produces them; ranking or blaming the workers changes nothing; only management changing the system does (paraphrase, Deming Institute).
- Know your variation. Some variation is ordinary noise in a stable process, some has a specific cause. Reacting to ordinary noise as if it meant something is "tampering", and it makes results worse (the funnel experiment, *Out of the Crisis* pp. 327 to 330, credited to Lloyd Nelson).
- Build quality in. Point 3: "Cease dependence on inspection to achieve quality. Eliminate the need for inspection on a mass basis by building quality into the product in the first place."
- Drive out fear (point 8). Eliminate slogans and numerical quotas (points 10 and 11).
- Not everything that matters can be counted. In *Out of the Crisis* (p. 121), crediting Lloyd Nelson, he wrote that the most important figures are unknown and unknowable, and that managing on visible figures alone fails (paraphrase).
- Plan, Do, Study, Act. He came to call the third step Study rather than Check, because "check" sounds like inspection rather than learning (paraphrase).
- Later framework, the System of Profound Knowledge: appreciation for a system, knowledge about variation, theory of knowledge, psychology.

**Quality bar.**
1. Every number shown has a known process behind it and a known range of ordinary variation.
2. A change is judged as signal or noise before anyone acts on it.
3. Quality is built in upstream, not inspected in at the end.
4. Nobody is ranked or blamed for what the system produces.
5. Every change has a written prediction before the test, and a note on what was learned after.

**Lens questions.**
- Is this change signal or noise? What is the normal range?
- What will a person do when they see this number, and would that be tampering?
- Is quality built in here, or are we relying on the gate to catch it?
- What matters here that cannot be measured, and are we pretending it can?
- What did we predict, and what did we learn?

**Applied to Atüned.**
- If the coherence number moves (hypothetical: 71 to 68) between sessions, the screen must not suggest the person got worse unless that move is outside the number's known ordinary variation. Know the range first, or do not show the change.
- A target such as "reach 80" is a numerical quota aimed at the person. In a stress app it invites gaming and adds pressure. The lens asks whether a goal belongs on screen at all.
- The gate is inspection. Deming would count it, then ask why 247 defects reached it. That count is a signal about the system that makes the work.

## Better candidates?

Lance decides. Each of these arguably belongs in product quality.

- **Shigeo Shingo.** *Zero Quality Control: Source Inspection and the Poka-Yoke System* (English edition 1986) argues that inspecting at the source plus mistake-proofing devices is the route to zero defects, with 112 shop-floor examples. It fits the gate directly: make the defect impossible, not just caught.
- **Edward Tufte.** *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* (1983) defines the "lie factor" (the size of an effect in the graphic compared with its size in the data), data-ink and chartjunk. It is a working test for how a real number is shown, which is Lance's rule 6.
- **Don Norman.** *The Design of Everyday Things*, first published in 1988 as *The Psychology of Everyday Things*, brought "affordance" into design and in its 2013 edition added "signifiers". It insists every action gets clear, timely feedback: a usability method the five above do not supply.

## 6. Ralph McQuarrie (animation, look and feel)

**Who and why.** Production illustrator whose paintings defined the look of *Star Wars*. Added by Emmy as the bar for an image that sells a whole world before it exists.

**Background.** Born 1929 in Gary, Indiana. After service in the Korean War he studied at the Art Center School in Los Angeles. Worked as a technical illustrator for Boeing (including diagrams for a 747 manual) and made animation for CBS News coverage of the Apollo missions at a small studio, Reel Three. The writers Hal Barwood and Matthew Robbins showed his work to George Lucas, who later asked him to illustrate his ideas for *Star Wars*. His paintings helped persuade studio executives to back the film, and a 1976 slide show of them was taken to conventions to build interest. He is credited with the look of Darth Vader, Chewbacca, the droids and many locations. Later work includes *Battlestar Galactica*, *Close Encounters of the Third Kind*, *Raiders of the Lost Ark* and *E.T.*, and he shared the Academy Award for visual effects for *Cocoon* (1985). He died in Berkeley on March 3, 2012.

**How he thinks.**
- A frame from the film, not a design sheet. The Boston Globe noted that most science fiction art of the time was for posters and book covers, while his looked like images you could see on the big screen. Doug Chiang called his work "cinematic".
- The image is the brief. Lucas said that when words could not carry an idea, he could point at one of Ralph's paintings and say "Do it like this" (via The Portalist).
- An engineer's eye. Years of technical illustration show in machines and costumes that look as if they work (interpretation from his career, not his own words).
- Serving the film. He is quoted saying "I just did my best to depict what I thought the film should look like" (via Wikipedia, secondary).
- Fast, committed images. One account says his key early paintings took a day or two each, mostly in gouache (secondary).

**Quality bar.**
1. One image carries the whole world: a stranger understands the story from the frame.
2. It looks photographed, not designed: light with a source, atmosphere, readable scale.
3. Machines, interfaces and costumes look like they function.
4. A builder can point at it and say "do it like this".

**Lens questions.**
- If this screen were a still from the finished film, what story does it tell?
- Where does the light come from, and does it mean something?
- Does scale read: what is large, what is near, what matters most?
- Does the machinery look like it works?
- Could a developer build it from this one image?

**Applied to Atüned.**
- The body map should read as a lit place with depth, not a diagram floating on black. Let light come from something that means something, such as the address carrying the most stress.
- Make one production painting of the core moment (a person seeing their coherence number change) before adding screens. Pin it up. It becomes the image everyone points at.
- This is the lens closest to Lance's own trade. Use it on motion keyframes too, not only stills.

## 7. Stephen Porges (behavioral sciences)

**Who and why.** Psychophysiologist who proposed polyvagal theory, the most widely used model of stress states in somatic practice. Added by Emmy because Atüned speaks about stress and the body. The scientific criticism is part of the lens, not a footnote.

**Background.** Introduced the theory in his presidential address to the Society for Psychophysiological Research in Atlanta on October 8, 1994, published as "Orienting in a defensive world: Mammalian modifications of our evolutionary heritage. A Polyvagal Theory", *Psychophysiology* 32(4), 1995, pp. 301 to 318. Distinguished University Scientist at the Kinsey Institute, Indiana University; professor of psychiatry at the University of North Carolina; emeritus at the University of Illinois at Chicago and the University of Maryland (per his podcast biography). Holds patents on technology for monitoring and regulating autonomic state. The Safe and Sound Protocol, a listening intervention built on his work, is distributed commercially by Unyte Health.

**How he thinks.**
- Three states in a hierarchy: a ventral vagal state that supports social engagement and safety; sympathetic mobilisation (fight or flight); a dorsal vagal state of immobilisation or shutdown.
- Neuroception, his term: the nervous system detects safety or danger without conscious awareness. In an interview he likened it to a high-level reflex (paraphrase).
- Safety first. Cues from face, voice and tone of voice signal safety and let defences come down; behaviour follows state (paraphrase).
- Measurement. He used respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA: the heart speeding and slowing with each breath) as an index of vagal influence on the heart.

**The criticism, stated plainly.**
- Paul Grossman (*Biological Psychology*, 2023) argues each of the theory's five basic premises is untenable or highly implausible. He calls treating RSA as general vagal tone a category mistake: it confuses a rough index with the thing itself.
- RSA is shaped by breathing rate, breath depth and other factors, so it is not a clean read of vagal output.
- Critics, including neuroanatomists Neuhuber and Berthoud (2022), dispute the anatomy and the evolutionary story; reported examples include vagal heart pathways in non-mammals such as lungfish (both reached through secondary summaries).
- A multi-author critique led by Grossman appeared in *Clinical Neuropsychiatry* in 2026. Porges replied in the same issue that it attacks claims the theory does not make, and that the theory remains open to testing and revision (paraphrase).
- A 2023 commentary summed up the split as "scientifically questionable but useful in practice". Controlled evidence for the Safe and Sound Protocol is limited, and critics raise conflict-of-interest questions.

**Quality bar.**
1. The interface itself cues safety: pace, tone, no alarm signals for ordinary states.
2. No physiological claim beyond what is actually measured.
3. State words (safe, mobilised, shut down) are labelled as a model, and the dispute is acknowledged.
4. Commercial claims are kept apart from evidence.
