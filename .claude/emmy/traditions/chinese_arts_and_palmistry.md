## 0. The name Lance used

Lance said "Xianmiang." The two likely targets are the same art:

- **面相 mian xiang**, "face appearance," the standard name for Chinese face reading. The book uses this spelling: "Chinese face reading, Mian Xiang" (line 7428).
- **相面 xiang mian**, "to read a face," the verb form. "Xianmiang" is probably this phrase heard aloud.

Both belong to **相術 xiang shu**, "the art of appraising appearance," which in China covered the face, bones, body, voice, gait and hands. Palm reading (**手相 shou xiang**) is a branch of it. Other spellings in English books: Mien Shiang, Mian Shiang. [R]

## 1. Chinese face reading (Mian Xiang)

### 1.1 Origin and history

- **Earliest records.** The Zuo zhuan (a chronicle compiled around the 4th century BCE) tells of a court official reading the faces of a nobleman's two sons, set in 626 BCE. Face reading is attested at least from the Spring and Autumn period (770 to 476 BCE). [R; Spring and Autumn dating V]
- **A profession by the Warring States period (475 to 221 BCE).** Political competition made physiognomers sought after. [V]
- **The first critique.** Xunzi (c. 310 to after 238 BCE) wrote a chapter titled **非相 Fei Xiang, "Against Physiognomy."** He names two famous readers, Gubu Ziqing and Tang Ju, and argues that "judging the outer form is not as good as judging the mind, and judging the mind is not as good as judging the method." A good person can have an ugly face; a petty person can have a handsome one. [V]
- **Han dynasty (206 BCE to 220 CE).** Physiognomy became more systematic and split into two branches, face and body. Wang Chong (27 to c. 97 CE) and Wang Fu (c. 82 to 167 CE) wrote about it. [V] Wang Chong's chapter "Bone Physiognomy" (骨相 Gu xiang) argued that fate shows in bone structure. The female reader Xu Fu appears in the Shiji reading the faces of future nobles. [R]
- **Indian and Buddhist influence.** A 2026 study argues that at least one branch of Chinese physiognomy was shaped by Indic models, carried by Buddhist translations. [V] Buddhist texts also brought the Indian list of the 32 marks of a great man. [R]
- **Tang dynasty manuscripts.** Physiognomy handbooks survive among the Dunhuang manuscripts, for example Pelliot chinois 2572 in the Bibliothèque nationale de France. [V that the item is catalogued as a physiognomy text]
- **Song to Ming: the classic handbooks.** **麻衣神相 Mayi Shenxiang**, "Divine Physiognomy of the Hemp-Robed One," is credited to a Daoist recluse of the early Song and to his student Chen Tuan (d. 989); the surviving text is a later compilation. Yuan Gong (1335 to 1410) and his son Yuan Zhongche (1376 to 1458) were Ming court physiognomers; **神相全編 Shenxiang Quanbian**, "Complete Compendium of Divine Physiognomy," is attributed to Yuan Zhongche. [R] The Ming encyclopedia **三才圖會 Sancai Tuhui** (published 1609 [R]) carries diagrams of the Twelve Palaces and the Five Officials. [V]
- **Modern.** Still widely practiced in China, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Southeast Asia and the diaspora. Western teachers include Lillian Bridges and Jean Haner; Joey Yap (Malaysia) teaches the Five Officials, Twelve Palaces and the 100-year map. [V for Yap; R for the others] AI face-reading apps marketed on "3,000 years of Mian Xiang" appeared in the 2020s. [V that they exist; their claims are unverified]

**Honest dating.** Documented history runs about 2,600 years. Claims of older origins are legend. The book's "thousands of years" (line 7428) fits the documented record.

### 1.2 Core structure

**The Three Stops, 三停 san ting.** The face divided horizontally into three. [V]

| Stop | Span | Life period | Classical triad |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upper | hairline to eyebrows | early life (youth) | Heaven |
| Middle | eyebrows to nose tip | middle life | Human |
| Lower | below the nose to chin | late life | Earth |

Sources differ on the lower boundary (nose tip or mouth). [V] The Heaven, Human, Earth labels come from the "three powers" (三才 san cai) found across Chinese cosmology. [R]

**The Five Officials, 五官 wu guan.** Five features, each an "official" with a job. The Chinese names agree across sources. [V]

| Feature | Official | Plain meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Ears | 采聽官 cai ting guan | Official of Hearing (gathering in) |
| Eyebrows | 保壽官 bao shou guan | Protector of Longevity |
| Eyes | 監察官 jian cha guan | Inspector (supervision, seeing) |
| Nose | 審辨官 shen bian guan | Judge (discernment) |
| Mouth | 出納官 chu na guan | Official of Intake and Outflow (the treasurer) |

**The Twelve Palaces, 十二宮 shi'er gong.** Twelve zones, each tied to one area of life. The names are fixed in the dictionary tradition. [V names] Locations: the first seven below are [V]; the rest are [R].

| Palace | Chinese | Location |
|---|---|---|
| Life | 命宮 ming gong | between the eyebrows (印堂 yintang); the most important single spot [V] |
| Wealth | 財帛 caibo | nose tip and wings [V] |
| Siblings | 兄弟 xiongdi | eyebrows [V] |
| Property (fields and houses) | 田宅 tianzhai | upper eyelids, between brow and eye [V] |
| Children | 男女 nannü | below the eyes [V] |
| Spouse | 妻妾 qiqie | outer corners of the eyes [V] |
| Health / Illness | 疾厄 ji'e | bridge of the nose between the eyes (山根 shan gen) [V] |
| Servants / Subordinates | 奴僕 nupu | chin and jaw corners [R] |
| Travel / Migration | 遷移 qianyi | temples and upper sides of the forehead [R] |
| Career / Office | 官祿 guanlu | center of the forehead [R] |
| Fortune and Virtue | 福德 fude | above the outer brows, toward the temples [R] |
| Appearance | 相貌 xiangmao | the face as a whole [R] |

Some texts add a Parents palace (父母宮) on the forehead corners called the Sun Horn (left) and Moon Horn (right). [R] Do not confuse these face palaces with the twelve palaces of Zi Wei Dou Shu, a separate astrology chart system. [V]

**Five Mountains and Four Rivers, 五嶽四瀆.** The face as a landscape. Mountains: forehead, chin, nose (center), left and right cheekbones. Rivers: ears, eyes, mouth, nostrils. Mountains should be full and "facing each other"; rivers should be clear and well formed. [R]

**Face shapes.** Two classical sets.

- **Ten character shapes, 十字面相.** Each named by a Chinese character whose outline resembles the face: 由 You, 甲 Jia, 申 Shen, 田 Tian, 同 Tong, 王 Wang, 圓 Yuan, 目 Mu, 用 Yong, 風 Feng. [V list] 田 is square; 甲 has a broad forehead and small chin; 由 has a small forehead and wide chin; 圓 is round. [V] 申 (narrow forehead and chin, wide cheekbones) is often called diamond-shaped; 王 (king) is bony with prominent cheekbones; 目 is long and narrow; 同 is evenly rectangular. [R]
- **Five-phase shapes.** Wood (long, upright), Fire (pointed, angular), Earth (thick, broad, heavy lower face), Metal (square, firm), Water (round, soft). [R] Descriptions of Fire vary: Chinese sources often describe it as narrow above and wide below; modern Western books often describe a pointed chin. [R]

**The 100-year map, 流年部位 liu nian bu wei.** Ages assigned to positions on the face, starting at the ears for childhood, moving down the center of the forehead from about 15, through the brows, eyes and nose in midlife, to the mouth and chin in old age. A reader looks at the position for a person's current age. [V that the map exists; R for the sequence]

**Complexion, 氣色 qi se.** Color and luster that change over days and months, read for timing: what is happening now, as against what the bones say over a lifetime. [R]

**Spirit, 神 shen.** Read mainly through the eyes. Classical rule: bright, steady eyes outweigh poor features. [R]

### 1.3 How it sees

This is the way of thinking Emmy can borrow.

1. **Three timescales on one surface.** Bones and structure are the long record. Features carry the life story. Complexion is the weather of the present. A reading always asks which timescale a sign belongs to.
2. **Map, then position, then quality.** First locate (which zone, which palace). Then read quality (full or thin, bright or dull, balanced or crooked). A sign means nothing until it is placed.
3. **The face as landscape.** Mountains, rivers, palaces. A face is read the way a geomancer reads land: support, flow, proportion.
4. **Balance over beauty.** The ideal is three stops of equal height and features in proportion, not attractiveness.
5. **No single sign decides.** Classical teachers warn against reading one feature alone. Spirit in the eyes can override a weak structure.
6. **Appearance follows the heart-mind.** Two common sayings: 相由心生, "appearance is born from the heart-mind," and 相隨心轉, "appearance turns as the heart-mind turns." [R] The tradition holds that the face changes as the person changes.

## 2. Chinese astrology: the twelve animals, stems and branches, BaZi

### 2.1 Origin and history

- **Stems and branches are older than the animals.** The ten Heavenly Stems and twelve Earthly Branches combine into a 60-step cycle (干支 ganzhi). Shang dynasty oracle bones (c. 1250 to 1050 BCE) already use it to count days. [R] Using it to count years became standard in the Han. [R]
- **The animals.** The earliest systematic list of branch animals is in the Qin "Day Books" (日書 Rishu) from Shuihudi Tomb 11, Hubei, a tomb sealed in 217 BCE. Several animals differ from the modern set (for example a deer at Wu where the Horse now sits); Rat, Ox, Tiger, Rabbit and Pig already match. Similar lists come from Fangmatan, Gansu (excavated 1986). The first full list matching the modern one is in Wang Chong's *Lunheng* (1st century CE). [V]
- **Fate calculation from birth time.** Li Xuzhong (Tang dynasty, 761 to 813 [R dates]) read fate from three pillars: year, month, day. Xu Ziping (late Five Dynasties to early Song, 10th century) added the hour as a fourth pillar and made the **Day Master** (the stem of the day pillar) the center of the reading. The method is still called 子平 Ziping after him. The first systematic book, **淵海子平 Yuanhai Ziping**, was compiled in the Song and revised in the Ming. [V] Later classics include *Ziping Zhenquan* (Shen Xiaozhan, Qing) and *Di Tian Sui* (annotated by Ren Tieqiao, Qing). [R]
- **A separate system.** Zi Wei Dou Shu (Purple Star Astrology) uses a chart of twelve palaces built from the birth moment. It is distinct from BaZi and from the face's twelve palaces. [V]
- **Spread.** Korea, Japan and Vietnam use the same cycle. Vietnam has the Cat in place of the Rabbit and the Water Buffalo for the Ox. [R]

**The 60-cycle.** Stems and branches advance together and only same-polarity pairs meet, so the cycle is 60, not 120. Jia-Zi is the first pair. [R]

**When the year begins.** Popular zodiac years start at Lunar New Year. BaZi starts the year at 立春 Lichun, the solar term around February 4, and starts each month at a solar term. [R]

**The BaZi chart (八字, "eight characters").** [V for the frame; R for the details]

- Four pillars: year, month, day, hour. Each pillar is one stem over one branch. Eight characters in all.
- **Day Master:** the day stem. It stands for the self. Everything else is read in relation to it.
- **Ten Gods (十神):** the relationship of every other character to the Day Master, by phase and polarity: Companion and Rob Wealth (same phase), Eating God and Hurting Officer (what the self produces), Direct and Indirect Wealth (what the self controls), Direct Officer and Seven Killings (what controls the self), Direct and Indirect Resource (what produces the self).
- **Hidden stems:** each branch contains one to three stems.
- **Interactions:** combinations, clashes (branches six apart, for example Rat and Horse), harms and punishments.
- **Strength and the useful element (用神 yong shen):** is the Day Master strong or weak, and which phase brings the chart toward balance.
- **Luck pillars (大運 da yun):** ten-year periods that unfold from the month pillar. Annual pillars are layered on top.

**The popular animal layer.** Character sketches for each birth-year animal and compatibility charts. This is the part most people know; BaZi practitioners treat the year animal as a small part of a chart.

### 2.3 How it sees

1. **A person is a configuration of qi at a moment.** Not a list of traits. Eight characters in tension.
2. **Everything is relative to the self-point.** The Day Master is the reference; the same character means different things in different charts.
3. **Balance, not good or bad.** A chart is read for excess and lack. The remedy is the missing phase.
4. **Time as layered cycles.** Hours inside days inside months inside years inside ten-year luck periods inside the 60-year cycle. A life is read as the natal pattern meeting each passing period.
5. **Interaction over item.** Clashes and combinations between characters carry the reading, much as the I Ching reads the relation between lines.

## 3. The I Ching (易經 Yijing, Book of Changes)

### 3.1 Origin and history

- **Core text, the Zhouyi (周易).** A divination manual of the Western Zhou, roughly 1000 to 750 BCE. 64 hexagrams, each with a name, a judgment, and six line statements. In its earliest layer it has little moral content. [V]
- **Traditional authorship (legend, not history).** Fu Xi drew the eight trigrams; King Wen doubled them into 64 and wrote the judgments; the Duke of Zhou wrote the line texts; Confucius wrote the Ten Wings. [R] Modern scholarship treats these as attributions.
- **Ten Wings (十翼).** Commentaries added from the Warring States into the early Han. They turned a divination manual into a philosophy of change. The Han canonized the combined work as one of the Five Classics. [V] The Wings are: Tuan (2 parts), Xiang (2), Xici or Great Treatise (2), Wenyan, Shuogua, Xugua, Zagua. [R]
- **Excavated manuscripts.** Mawangdui silk manuscript, tomb sealed 168 BCE, with a different hexagram order and extra commentaries. [V] Shanghai Museum Chu bamboo manuscript (c. 300 BCE) and Fuyang Han slips (c. 165 BCE). [R] Edward Shaughnessy, *Unearthing the Changes* (Columbia, 2014), studies these. [V]
- **Commentary traditions.** Wang Bi (226 to 249) read it for meaning and principle (義理 yili). Shao Yong (1011 to 1077) worked out number-and-image diagrams, including the "Fuxi" order. Zhu Xi (1130 to 1200) insisted it was first a divination book and wrote a standard guide to consulting it. [R; Shao Yong attribution V]
- **To the West.** Jesuit Joachim Bouvet sent Leibniz a woodcut of the Fuxi hexagram order from Beijing on 4 November 1701; Leibniz saw his binary arithmetic in it. A critic questioned the link as early as 1704. [V] James Legge's English translation (1882) and Richard Wilhelm's German (1924; English by Cary Baynes, 1950, with a foreword by C. G. Jung) made it widely known. Jung linked it to his idea of synchronicity. [R]

### 3.2 Core structure

- **Lines:** a solid line (yang, ⚊) and a broken line (yin, ⚋).
- **Eight trigrams (八卦 bagua):** three lines each. [R]

| Trigram | Name | Image | Quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| ☰ | Qian | Heaven | creative, strong |

- **64 hexagrams:** two trigrams stacked, six lines, read bottom to top. 384 line statements in all. [R]
- **Two classic arrangements of the trigrams:** Earlier Heaven (Fuxi) and Later Heaven (King Wen). The Later Heaven order is used in feng shui and in Chinese palm reading. [R]
- **Positions inside a hexagram:** line 1 is the beginning, line 6 the end; lines 2 and 5 are the centers of the lower and upper trigrams; lines 1 and 4, 2 and 5, 3 and 6 "respond" to each other. The top two lines are Heaven, the middle two Human, the bottom two Earth. [R]
- **Casting.** Yarrow stalks (50 stalks, one set aside, 49 divided repeatedly) or, from more than a thousand years later, three coins. Each cast gives 6, 7, 8 or 9. Seven and eight are stable lines. Six (old yin) and nine (old yang) are "changing lines" that flip into their opposite, producing a second hexagram that shows where the situation is going. [V] Odds differ by method: yarrow gives old yin 1/16, young yang 5/16, young yin 7/16, old yang 3/16; three coins give 1/8, 3/8, 3/8, 1/8. [V]
- **Generation sequence (Xici):** "In the Changes there is the Supreme Ultimate (太極 taiji). It gives rise to the Two Modes. The Two Modes give rise to the Four Images. The Four Images give rise to the Eight Trigrams." And: "One yin, one yang: that is called the Way." [R]

### 3.3 How it sees

1. **It reads a moment, not a person.** A hexagram is the shape of a situation, and every situation is already moving.
2. **Position is meaning.** The same line means different things in different places: low or high, central or not, answered by its partner line or not.
3. **Extremes turn.** A line pushed to its limit (old yin, old yang) becomes its opposite. Change is built into the structure.
4. **Timing over outcome.** Most line texts are about when to act, when to wait, when to stop. "Good fortune" and "misfortune" usually depend on conduct.
5. **The question shapes the answer.** The practice requires a sincere, specific question. The reading is a mirror held at an angle.

## 4. Taoist cosmology and qi: yin and yang, Wu Xing, meridians, dantian

### 4.1 Origin and history

- **Yin and yang.** The words first meant the shady and sunny sides of a hill. They became paired cosmic principles in the Warring States. Zou Yan (c. 305 to 240 BCE) is the name tied to the yin-yang and five-phase school. [R]
- **Five phases (五行 wu xing).** First listed in the "Great Plan" (Hongfan) chapter of the Book of Documents: water, fire, wood, metal, earth. Han thinkers (Dong Zhongshu and others) built them into a full system of correspondences. "Phases" is preferred to "elements" because they are stages of change, not substances. [R]
- **Laozi and the Daodejing.** Traditionally by Laozi, 6th century BCE; his historicity is uncertain. The text took shape by about the 4th century BCE. The oldest copy is the partial Guodian bamboo version (tomb c. 300 BCE); two silk copies came from Mawangdui. 81 short chapters. [R] The Zhuangzi (core chapters by Zhuang Zhou, c. 369 to 286 BCE) is the second foundation. [R]
- **Religious Daoism** from the Celestial Masters (2nd century CE) onward; **inner alchemy (內丹 neidan)** develops in the Tang and Song; the Daoist canon was printed in 1445. [R]
- **Chinese medicine.** The Mawangdui medical manuscripts (copied about 215 BCE, tomb sealed 168 BCE) describe **eleven** vessels, not twelve, and treat them by cauterization (moxa), not needles. [V] The **Huangdi Neijing** (Yellow Emperor's Inner Classic), in two parts, *Suwen* and *Lingshu*, was compiled around the 1st century BCE and gives the twelve-channel system. [V] Its "Yellow Emperor" authorship is legend. Huangfu Mi's *Systematic Classic of Acupuncture and Moxibustion* (3rd century CE) fixed point locations; Wang Weiyi cast bronze teaching figures in 1027. [R]
- **"Meridian"** is a Western coinage. The Chinese 經 jing means a channel or warp thread. The French diplomat George Soulié de Morant popularized "méridien" in the 1930s. [V]
- **Qigong** as a word is modern (popularized in 1950s China), though breathing and movement practices are ancient; the Mawangdui tomb held a painted chart of 導引 daoyin exercises. [R]

### 4.2 Core structure

**Yin and yang.** [R]

- Opposed and complementary: night and day, cold and hot, still and moving, inner and outer, descending and rising.
- Each contains the seed of the other.
- Each turns into the other at its extreme.
- They are relative: a thing is yin or yang only in relation to something else.
- The familiar two-fish swirl symbol is late (Ming, 14th to 16th century). The Song thinker Zhou Dunyi's 11th-century Taiji diagram was a different, stacked design.

**Five phases, with the standard medical correspondences** (*Suwen* chapter 5 and related chapters). [R]

| Phase | Yin organ | Yang organ | Emotion | Season | Color | Sense opening |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wood | Liver | Gallbladder | anger (怒) | spring | green-blue | eyes |
| Fire | Heart | Small Intestine | joy (喜) | summer | red | tongue |
| Earth | Spleen | Stomach | pensiveness, worry (思) | late summer | yellow | mouth |
| Metal | Lung | Large Intestine | grief, sorrow (悲, 憂) | autumn | white | nose |
| Water | Kidney | Bladder | fear (恐) | winter | black | ears |

- **Generating cycle (相生):** Wood feeds Fire, Fire makes Earth (ash), Earth bears Metal, Metal carries Water (condensation), Water nourishes Wood.
- **Controlling cycle (相剋):** Wood parts Earth, Earth dams Water, Water quenches Fire, Fire melts Metal, Metal cuts Wood.

**Qi and the Three Treasures (三寶).** Qi (氣) is breath, vapor, the moving stuff of life and world. Jing (精), essence, stored in the kidneys and lower body. Qi, active energy. Shen (神), spirit, housed in the heart and seen in the eyes. Inner alchemy's formula: refine jing into qi, qi into shen, shen back into emptiness (煉精化氣，煉氣化神，煉神還虛). [R]

**The twelve primary meridians (十二正經).** Each is named by limb (hand or foot), a yin-yang grade, and an organ. Listed in the order qi is said to circulate, with the two-hour "organ clock" peak. [R; counts and names are standard]

- **Eight extraordinary vessels (奇經八脈):** Governor (Du, back midline, 28 points), Conception (Ren, front midline, 24 points), Penetrating (Chong), Belt (Dai), Yin and Yang Heel (Qiao), Yin and Yang Linking (Wei). Only Du and Ren have their own points. [R]

- Plus connecting (luo), divergent, sinew and cutaneous channels, and many "extra" points. [R]

**Points relevant to MOB's charge circuit.** [R]

- **勞宮 Laogong, PC-8, "Palace of Labor":** center of the palm.
- **湧泉 Yongquan, KI-1, "Gushing Spring":** sole of the foot, front third. The first point of the Kidney channel.
- **百會 Baihui, GV-20, "Hundred Meetings":** the crown.
- **膻中 Shanzhong, CV-17:** center of the chest between the nipples; the classical meeting point of qi.
- **印堂 Yintang:** between the brows (also the face-reading Life Palace).

**The dantian (丹田, "elixir field").** [R]

- Three fields: **lower** (below the navel and inward, the root of jing and the main focus of qigong and martial arts), **middle** (the chest at heart level; some traditions place it at the solar plexus), **upper** (between and behind the brows; the "Niwan" or mud-ball palace in the brain).
- The term appears in Daoist sources from about the 2nd to 3rd century CE and is central in the *Huangting jing* (Yellow Court Scripture, c. 3rd to 4th century). Japanese practice calls the lower field hara or tanden.
- Two classical practice phrases: 五心朝天, "five hearts face heaven," a seated posture with both palms, both soles and the crown (or heart) open upward; and 意到氣到, "where intention goes, qi arrives."

### 4.3 How it sees

1. **Pattern, not cause.** Chinese medicine names a pattern (for example "liver qi stagnation") from many signs together, rather than one cause for one symptom.
2. **Flow and obstruction.** A common teaching maxim, later than the classics: 通則不痛，痛則不通, "where there is free flow there is no pain; where there is pain there is no free flow." [R]
3. **Correspondence thinking.** Organs, emotions, seasons, colors, tastes and directions are linked in fives. A change in one domain is checked against the others.
4. **Four examinations.** Look (face, tongue, posture), listen and smell, ask, touch (pulse at the wrist, palpation). [R]
5. **Balance and timing.** Too much and too little are both disease. Treatment follows the hour, season and constitution.
6. **Wu wei (無為), non-forcing action.** "The Way never acts, yet nothing is left undone" (Daodejing 37). "Reversal is the movement of the Way" (Daodejing 40). "Being and non-being produce each other" (Daodejing 2). [R]
7. **One before two.** "The Way gives birth to One. One gives birth to Two. Two gives birth to Three. Three gives birth to the ten thousand things." (Daodejing 42) [R]

## 5. Palmistry across cultures

Nothing in this section was web-verified this session; the search budget ran out before it. Everything is [R]. Check before stating as settled.

### 5.1 Origin and history

**India: Samudrika Shastra and hasta rekha.** [R]

- **Samudrika Shastra** (सामुद्रिक शास्त्र), "the science of body marks," reads the whole body: face, gait, voice, hands, feet. Palm reading within it is **hasta samudrika** or **hasta rekha shastra** ("science of hand lines"). The name is traditionally tied to a sage called Samudra or to the ocean; the origin of the name is uncertain.
- **Earliest datable layers.** Buddhist texts list the 32 marks of a great man, including wheel marks on the palms and soles (Lakkhana Sutta, Digha Nikaya 30; the Pali canon was written down around the 1st century BCE). Varahamihira's *Brihat Samhita* (c. 550 CE) has chapters on the marks of men and women, including the hands. The *Samudrika Tilaka* (Durlabharaja, 12th century, Gujarat) and the Jain *Hasta Sanjivana* (Meghavijaya, 17th century) are later handbooks.
- **Popular claims** of a 5,000-year Indian origin are not documented. Datable texts begin around the turn of the common era.
- **Reading conventions.** Traditionally the right hand for men and the left for women. Auspicious marks: fish, conch, lotus, wheel (chakra), trident, flag. Line names in common use: Jeevan or Ayu rekha (life), Mastishka rekha (head), Hridaya rekha (heart), Bhagya rekha (fate).

**China: shou xiang (手相).** [R]

- Part of the wider physiognomy tradition (section 1). Hand chapters appear in the Song and Ming compendia.
- Hands read for shape (five phases), color, texture, fingers, lines, and fingerprint patterns. Folk rhymes count whorls and loops on the fingertips.
- Men read on the left, women on the right (男左女右).

**The West: chiromancy and chirognomy.** [R]

- **Antiquity.** Aristotle (*History of Animals*, Book I) notes that long-lived people have one or two lines running right across the palm. Later writers built a pseudo-Aristotelian palmistry on this.
- **Medieval.** Latin chiromancy texts appear from the 12th century; one is reported in the Eadwine Psalter (c. 1160).
- **Renaissance print.** Johannes Hartlieb, *Die Kunst Ciromantia* (written c. 1448, printed as a block book c. 1475); Bartolomeo della Rocca ("Cocles"), *Chyromantie ac physionomie anastasis* (1504); Johannes ab Indagine (1522). Planetary names for the mounts become standard. The church and courts repeatedly condemned it as divination.
- **19th-century revival.** Casimir d'Arpentigny, *La Chirognomonie* (1839), classifies hand shapes. Adolphe Desbarrolles, *Les Mystères de la main* (1859), revives line reading. In England, the Vagrancy Act of 1824 named palmistry among prosecutable fortune-telling practices.
- **Cheiro** (William John Warner, 1866 to 1936), Irish. *Cheiro's Language of the Hand* (1894) and later books. He read for celebrities and claimed training from Brahmins in India; that claim is his own and unverified.
- **Others.** Katharine St. Hill (Chirological Society, London, 1889); William G. Benham, *The Laws of Scientific Hand Reading* (1900); Charlotte Wolff, a psychiatrist who studied hands clinically (*The Human Hand*, 1942; *The Hand in Psychological Diagnosis*, 1951); Fred Gettings, *The Book of the Hand* (1965), source of the four elemental hand types.

Also read: thumb (will and logic in its two phalanges), fingers, nails, skin texture, flexibility, and marks (stars, crosses, triangles, islands, grilles).

**Chinese: the palm as a bagua.** [R] The eight trigrams are laid around the palm, with **明堂 Mingtang** (the Bright Hall) in the center: Gen at the base of the thumb, Zhen above it on the thumb side, Xun below the index finger, Li below the middle finger, Kun below the ring and little fingers, Dui on the outer edge, Qian at the lower outer edge, Kan at the wrist center. Exact placements vary by school.

**Chinese: the three main lines.** [R] 天紋 Heaven line (the heart line), 人紋 Human line (the head line), 地紋 Earth line (the life line).

**Reading which hand.** Western practice reads the non-dominant hand as inherited potential and the dominant hand as what the person has made of it, and compares the two. India and China traditionally read by sex. [R]

### 5.3 How it sees

1. **Shape first, lines last.** Temperament from the hand's shape and feel, then fingers, then mounts, then lines, then marks.
2. **Two hands, two times.** The comparison of both hands is the tradition's way of separating what was given from what was built.
3. **Lines change.** Most modern practitioners hold that minor lines change over years, and teach the hand as a record, not a sentence.
4. **No single sign decides.** A mark is read only when other features confirm it.
5. **Planetary grammar (West) or trigram grammar (China).** Each region of the palm carries a domain, so a line's meaning depends on where it starts and ends.

### 5.5 Status

- **Documented:** Indian body-mark lore from the late first millennium BCE in Buddhist texts; Indian handbooks from the 6th century CE; Western chiromancy from the 12th century; the modern Western system from the 19th century. [R]
- **The tradition holds:** the hand shows character, health and life course.
- **Tested:** [R]
  - Palm flexion creases form before birth, roughly in weeks 7 to 14 of pregnancy. Ridge patterns (fingerprints) are fixed by about week 24. This fits "readable at birth" for the major creases. Creases also deepen and multiply with use and age, which fits "across a lifetime."
  - Dermatoglyphics (ridge patterns) has real medical uses. A single transverse crease is more common in Down syndrome and some other conditions, but also occurs in the general population.
  - Life line length and age at death: a 1974 autopsy study found no correlation; a 1990 study of 100 patients reported one, but skin and creases change with age, which confounds it.
  - No controlled study supports palmistry's claims about character or fate.
  - Line 7454's "neurologically mapped to more cortical surface than any other region": Penfield's sensory and motor maps give the largest areas to the hand and to the face and mouth together. [R] Worth stating precisely.
