# ATUNED — Core 10, Three-Register Funnel Quiz (V1.1)

**Status:** All 30 questions revised for **0–10 self-report**, not yet wired into the live funnel or canonical CQ.  
**Source:** `atuned_src/engine/data/canon.js#SI`. Exactly 10 of the existing 21 laws; all seven bands represented.

## The measurement rule

**Three questions per law are all answerable 0–10.** Each question names (a) a concrete situation, (b) an observable action, and (c) the frequency of that action **when the situation actually occurred**.

- **Behavior (aligned)** — how often I took the constructive action under ordinary relevant circumstances.
- **Pressure (aligned)** — how often I took the constructive action when it became difficult, but was still reasonably safe.
- **Mechanism (reverse-facing)** — how often a specific *protective action* happened in response to a specific perceived cost (e.g., withholding the truth to keep approval). This measures the frequency of **that proposed mechanism**, not proof that it is the person's true root cause.

**Anchors for each:** `0 = Never`, `5 = About half the eligible opportunities`, `10 = Always`. **Not applicable / no such situation** or **Prefer not to answer** is a null value, not zero. The third score has a different direction: **10 means the protective behavior happens frequently**, not that the person has high integrity. Do not average all three registers as if they were the same measure. Keep the raw values separate and interpret each one according to its polarity.

Use the optional follow-up “Is this what was happening for you?” and allow **Another reason, in my own words**. The question's explanation must never be forced. Respect contexts of privacy, actual danger, inability, or coercion without labeling them as failures.

## The 30 questions and 10 learning stories

### 01. Truth · Throat

**1. Behavior · 0–10 · aligned**

When someone asked you for an honest answer about something important, how often did you tell the truth?

**2. Pressure · 0–10 · aligned**

When telling the truth could disappoint someone, how often did you still give an honest answer when it was safe?

**3. Mechanism · 0–10 · reverse-facing protective pattern**

When you worried someone would be upset by the truth, how often did you leave out an important fact to keep them happy?

What this tests: *Withholding an important fact to preserve approval*. If it did not happen, choose **Not applicable**. If the proposed reason is wrong, say so; the result cannot assert hidden motivation.

**Story — The promise you didn't mean**

A friend asks you to help this weekend. You already need rest, but you say yes to avoid disappointing them. By Saturday, you feel trapped and resentful.

**Constructive behavior:** You give an honest answer, even when it is uncomfortable. Both people can make a real choice.

**When the opposite happens:** You hide your limits to keep approval. The promise and your real intention drift apart.

**Release reflection:** Notice the belief that an honest no will cost connection.

**Becoming action:** Give one respectful, truthful answer about what you can actually do.

**Boundary:** Privacy, danger and coercion are not failures to practice truth.

### 02. Transparency · Throat

**1. Behavior · 0–10 · aligned**

When your choices affected another person, how often did you tell them the information they needed?

**2. Pressure · 0–10 · aligned**

When you made a mistake that affected someone else, how often did you tell them even if it was embarrassing?

**3. Mechanism · 0–10 · reverse-facing protective pattern**

When you worried a mistake would make someone think less of you, how often did you hide that mistake from the person it affected?

What this tests: *Hiding an important mistake to protect reputation*. If it did not happen, choose **Not applicable**. If the proposed reason is wrong, say so; the result cannot assert hidden motivation.

**Story — The mistake no one saw**

You spot an error in a shared project. Nobody else has noticed. If you say nothing, the team will make a decision using the wrong numbers.

**Constructive behavior:** You name the error and what it affects. Others can make informed choices and correct course.

**When the opposite happens:** You protect your image by hiding something relevant. People act on incomplete information.

**Release reflection:** Notice the worry that admitting a mistake will make you less valuable.

**Becoming action:** Share one necessary fact clearly with the person affected.

**Boundary:** Transparency is not an obligation to share private or unsafe information.

### 03. Unity · Crown

**1. Behavior · 0–10 · aligned**

When you disagreed with someone, how often did you still treat them with respect?

**2. Pressure · 0–10 · aligned**

When people you wanted to fit in with mocked someone, how often did you choose not to join in?

**3. Mechanism · 0–10 · reverse-facing protective pattern**

When you wanted a group's approval, how often did you join in putting down someone the group disliked?

What this tests: *Joining group ridicule to avoid social disapproval*. If it did not happen, choose **Not applicable**. If the proposed reason is wrong, say so; the result cannot assert hidden motivation.

**Story — The person everyone disagrees with**

A group conversation turns against someone who sees things differently. You don't agree with them, but you notice people are talking as if they matter less.

**Constructive behavior:** You keep your judgment of an idea separate from your respect for a person.

**When the opposite happens:** You join in treating a person as less worthy because they differ from your group.

**Release reflection:** Notice the need to belong that can overpower your own values.

**Becoming action:** Disagree with one idea without diminishing the person.

**Boundary:** Equal dignity doesn't require agreement or staying near someone unsafe.

### 04. Presence · 3rd Eye

**1. Behavior · 0–10 · aligned**

When someone was talking to you, how often did you pay attention to what they were saying?

**2. Pressure · 0–10 · aligned**

When worries distracted you during a conversation, how often did you bring your attention back to the person speaking?

**3. Mechanism · 0–10 · reverse-facing protective pattern**

When a conversation brought up an uncomfortable feeling, how often did you turn to your phone or another distraction to avoid that feeling?

What this tests: *Using distraction to avoid discomfort*. If it did not happen, choose **Not applicable**. If the proposed reason is wrong, say so; the result cannot assert hidden motivation.

**Story — The conversation you missed**

Someone close to you is telling you about a hard day. Your phone lights up. You keep checking it and miss what they actually needed.

**Constructive behavior:** You return your attention and understand more accurately what is being shared.

**When the opposite happens:** You leave the moment without noticing. Assumptions replace what was actually said.

**Release reflection:** Notice what pulls your attention away, without judging the distraction itself.

**Becoming action:** Put one distraction aside and listen long enough to reflect back what you heard.

**Boundary:** Pain, disability, nervous-system load and divided demands can limit attention; adjust expectations fairly.

### 05. Compassion · Heart

**1. Behavior · 0–10 · aligned**

When someone told you they were struggling, how often did you listen before responding?

**2. Pressure · 0–10 · aligned**

When you were tired and someone needed support, how often did you respond with care while respecting your own limits?

**3. Mechanism · 0–10 · reverse-facing protective pattern**

When someone's distress made you uncomfortable, how often did you change the subject to avoid hearing more about their pain?

What this tests: *Changing subject to avoid another's distress*. If it did not happen, choose **Not applicable**. If the proposed reason is wrong, say so; the result cannot assert hidden motivation.

**Story — Fixing when they needed listening**

A colleague tells you they're overwhelmed. You immediately list solutions. Later you realize they needed to be heard before they could think about next steps.

**Constructive behavior:** You make space for the person's actual needs while keeping your own limits.

**When the opposite happens:** You try to stop your discomfort by fixing or dismissing the other person.

**Release reflection:** Notice the urge to end discomfort quickly.

**Becoming action:** Ask once: 'Would listening or problem-solving help more?'

**Boundary:** Compassion does not require self-sacrifice, endless availability or ignoring boundaries.

### 06. Courage · Solar

**1. Behavior · 0–10 · aligned**

When something important needed to be said, how often did you speak up when it was reasonably safe?

**2. Pressure · 0–10 · aligned**

When speaking up might bring disapproval, how often did you still say what mattered when it was safe?

**3. Mechanism · 0–10 · reverse-facing protective pattern**

When you feared someone would reject you for speaking up, how often did you stay silent even though it was safe to speak?

What this tests: *Staying silent to avoid rejection when speaking is safe*. If it did not happen, choose **Not applicable**. If the proposed reason is wrong, say so; the result cannot assert hidden motivation.

**Story — The conversation you keep delaying**

A workplace issue keeps coming back. You know a conversation could help, but you rehearse it for days because you're worried how it will land.

**Constructive behavior:** You take a proportionate, reasonably safe step aligned with what matters.

**When the opposite happens:** You avoid every possible step and let an important issue continue without review.

**Release reflection:** Notice whether rejection, conflict or uncertainty is driving the delay.

**Becoming action:** Choose one small, safe action toward the conversation.

**Boundary:** Declining to enter danger is sound judgment, not lack of courage.

### 07. Duty · Solar

**1. Behavior · 0–10 · aligned**

When you freely made a realistic promise, how often did you follow through?

**2. Pressure · 0–10 · aligned**

When something unexpected prevented you from keeping a promise, how often did you tell the affected person before the deadline?

**3. Mechanism · 0–10 · reverse-facing protective pattern**

When you feared disappointing someone, how often did you agree to a task you already knew you could not reasonably finish?

What this tests: *Overcommitting to avoid disappointing someone*. If it did not happen, choose **Not applicable**. If the proposed reason is wrong, say so; the result cannot assert hidden motivation.

**Story — The yes that became too much**

You agree to finish a task by Friday. New demands pile up, and by Wednesday you know you won't finish. You consider staying silent.

**Constructive behavior:** You keep workable promises, or speak up early so the other person can adapt.

**When the opposite happens:** You hide an impossible commitment and let others discover the cost too late.

**Release reflection:** Notice the belief that disappointing someone makes you unworthy.

**Becoming action:** Renegotiate one commitment while there is still time.

**Boundary:** Forced obligations and unreasonable demands are not freely chosen duties.

### 08. Accountability · Solar

**1. Behavior · 0–10 · aligned**

When your mistake affected someone else, how often did you acknowledge what you did?

**2. Pressure · 0–10 · aligned**

When admitting a mistake could damage your reputation, how often did you still own your part?

**3. Mechanism · 0–10 · reverse-facing protective pattern**

When you feared being blamed for a mistake, how often did you make excuses instead of acknowledging your part?

What this tests: *Making excuses to avoid blame*. If it did not happen, choose **Not applicable**. If the proposed reason is wrong, say so; the result cannot assert hidden motivation.

**Story — The explanation before the apology**

You forget something that mattered to a friend. When they mention it, you immediately describe how busy you've been. They still haven't heard you own the impact.

**Constructive behavior:** You name what you did, acknowledge the effect, and offer a proportionate repair.

**When the opposite happens:** You use a true explanation to avoid taking your share of responsibility.

**Release reflection:** Notice the fear that admitting a mistake makes you fundamentally bad.

**Becoming action:** Say what happened and ask what repair would be useful, when appropriate.

**Boundary:** Taking your share does not mean accepting blame for someone else's actions.

### 09. Temperance · Sacral

**1. Behavior · 0–10 · aligned**

When you noticed you had enough of something you wanted, how often did you stop?

**2. Pressure · 0–10 · aligned**

When you were stressed, how often did you stop at enough instead of continuing just for relief?

**3. Mechanism · 0–10 · reverse-facing protective pattern**

When you felt upset, how often did you keep doing a comforting activity after you wanted to stop so you would not have to face the feeling?

What this tests: *Continuing a comforting activity to avoid a feeling*. If it did not happen, choose **Not applicable**. If the proposed reason is wrong, say so; the result cannot assert hidden motivation.

**Story — One more hour**

You keep scrolling late at night, looking for a little relief after a hard day. An hour later, you're more tired and the relief hasn't lasted.

**Constructive behavior:** You recognize what you actually need and choose an action that supports it.

**When the opposite happens:** You keep reaching for more even when it is no longer helping.

**Release reflection:** Notice whether comfort, avoidance or habit is behind the next reach.

**Becoming action:** Pause at a familiar moment and choose rest, connection or another real need.

**Boundary:** Do not label health conditions or basic needs as lack of temperance.

### 10. Non-Harm · Root

**1. Behavior · 0–10 · aligned**

When you were upset with someone, how often did you avoid saying or doing something meant to hurt them?

**2. Pressure · 0–10 · aligned**

When setting a boundary with someone who upset you, how often did you do it without trying to punish them?

**3. Mechanism · 0–10 · reverse-facing protective pattern**

When you felt disrespected, how often did you say something cutting because you wanted the other person to feel hurt too?

What this tests: *Hurting back to recover a sense of respect*. If it did not happen, choose **Not applicable**. If the proposed reason is wrong, say so; the result cannot assert hidden motivation.

**Story — The true but cutting reply**

Someone upsets you. You have an accurate criticism ready, but you also know the way you could say it would be needlessly cruel.

**Constructive behavior:** You state what is true and set a firm limit without adding unnecessary injury.

**When the opposite happens:** You use truth or silence as punishment and increase the damage.

**Release reflection:** Notice what part of you wants the other person to feel the pain you felt.

**Becoming action:** Express one boundary without humiliation or retaliation.

**Boundary:** Protecting yourself or others from immediate harm is not the same as unnecessary aggression.

## Interpret a three-register answer (illustration only)

Suppose a user gives Truth: **Behavior 8**, **Pressure 4**, **Mechanism 7**. The accurate reading is: *“You report being truthful often in ordinary situations. When honesty could disappoint someone, you report being truthful less often. You also report often leaving out an important fact when worried about upsetting someone.”* This does **not** establish dishonesty as an identity, a hidden motive as fact, or an authorized CQ correction. The mechanism is a self-reported defensive behavior, and the user may identify another explanation.
